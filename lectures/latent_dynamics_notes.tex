\section{Notation, from scratch}\label{sec:notation}

A \emph{vector} $\vx = (x_1, \dots, x_d)$ is just a list of $d$ numbers; we write
$\vx \in \reals^d$. Bold lowercase letters ($\vx, \vy, \veta$) are vectors,
capital letters ($C, \Sigma$) are matrices, and $A\trp$ is the transpose of $A$.
We write $I_n$ for the $n \times n$ \emph{identity matrix} (ones on the diagonal,
zeros elsewhere); the subscript names its size, e.g.\ $I_m$ and $I_d$ below. A
bare $I$ means the identity whose size is fixed by context.

The star of these notes is the \emph{Gaussian} (a.k.a.\ \emph{normal})
distribution---the bell curve. In one dimension, a random number $x$ with mean
$\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ has the familiar density
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gauss1d}
    p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}
           \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
\end{equation}
In $d$ dimensions the variance $\sigma^2$ is replaced by a $d \times d$
\emph{covariance matrix} $\Sigma$ (it records the variance of each coordinate and
the covariance between every pair), and the mean $\mu$ by a mean \emph{vector}
$\vm$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussmv}
    p(\vx) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^d \det \Sigma}}
             \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\vx-\vm)\trp \Sigma^{-1} (\vx-\vm)\right).
\end{equation}
Writing that mouthful every time is painful, so we abbreviate ``$\vx$ is Gaussian
with mean $\vm$ and covariance $\Sigma$'' as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalnotation}
    \vx \sim \Normal(\vm, \Sigma).
\end{equation}
The curly $\Normal$ is just shorthand for \textbf{N}ormal (the distribution is
named after Gauss); the ``$\sim$'' reads ``is distributed as.'' That is the only
new symbol you need---everything below is a statement about what happens to
$\vm$ and $\Sigma$.

\section{Gaussian fun facts}\label{sec:facts}

Both PPCA and factor analysis are \emph{linear-Gaussian}: the observation is a
linear image of a Gaussian latent, plus independent Gaussian noise. Remarkably,
two facts do all the work---how a Gaussian survives an affine map, and how a
jointly Gaussian pair marginalizes and conditions. Gaussians are the friendly
distribution: push them through linear algebra and they stay Gaussian. That
closure---that the result is \emph{still} Gaussian---is the standard property we
take as given (it follows cleanly from characteristic functions); the proofs
below then just pin down the resulting mean and covariance.

\begin{funfact}[Affine maps keep Gaussians Gaussian]\label{fact:affine}
Let $\vx \sim \Normal(\vm, \Sigma)$ and $\vy = A\vx + \vb$ for a fixed matrix
$A$ and vector $\vb$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:affine}
    \vy \sim \Normal(A\vm + \vb,\; A \Sigma A\trp).
\end{equation}
\end{funfact}

\begin{proof}
An affine map of a Gaussian is Gaussian, so it suffices to match the first two
moments. Linearity of expectation gives $\E[\vy] = A\E[\vx] + \vb = A\vm + \vb$.
For the covariance, write $\vy - \E[\vy] = A(\vx - \vm)$, hence
\begin{equation}
    \cov(\vy) = \E\!\left[A(\vx-\vm)(\vx-\vm)\trp A\trp\right]
              = A\,\E\!\left[(\vx-\vm)(\vx-\vm)\trp\right] A\trp
              = A \Sigma A\trp. \qedhere
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\emph{One dimension.} With scalars, $y = ax + b$ and $x \sim \Normal(\mu,
\sigma^2)$ give $y \sim \Normal(a\mu + b,\; a^2\sigma^2)$: shifting moves the
center, scaling by $a$ stretches the spread by $a^2$.

\begin{funfact}[Independent Gaussians add up]\label{fact:sum}
Let $\vx \sim \Normal(\vm_1, \Sigma_1)$ and $\vz \sim \Normal(\vm_2, \Sigma_2)$
be \emph{independent}. Then their sum is again Gaussian,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sum}
    \vx + \vz \sim \Normal(\vm_1 + \vm_2,\; \Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2).
\end{equation}
\end{funfact}

\begin{proof}
A sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian (stack them into
$\begin{psmallmatrix}\vx\\\vz\end{psmallmatrix}$, which is jointly Gaussian, and
apply \cref{fact:affine} with $A = [\,I \;\; I\,]$). Matching moments,
$\E[\vx+\vz] = \vm_1 + \vm_2$, and by independence the cross terms vanish,
\begin{equation}
    \cov(\vx + \vz) = \cov(\vx) + \cov(\vz) = \Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2. \qedhere
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\emph{One dimension.} Two independent bell curves add to a wider bell:
$\Normal(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $\Normal(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ sum to
$\Normal(\mu_1 + \mu_2,\; \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$---variances add, so noise
piles up.

Now suppose two vectors are Gaussian \emph{together}. Partition the stacked
vector and its mean and covariance into blocks,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:joint}
    \begin{pmatrix} \va \\ \vb \end{pmatrix}
    \sim \Normal\!\left(
        \begin{pmatrix} \vm_a \\ \vm_b \end{pmatrix},\;
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \Sigma_{aa} & \Sigma_{ab} \\
            \Sigma_{ba} & \Sigma_{bb}
        \end{pmatrix}
    \right),
    \qquad \Sigma_{ba} = \Sigma_{ab}\trp .
\end{equation}

\begin{funfact}[Marginalize and condition]\label{fact:cond}
For the jointly Gaussian pair \eqref{eq:joint}, the \emph{marginal} of $\va$ is
read straight off the blocks,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marginal}
    \va \sim \Normal(\vm_a,\; \Sigma_{aa}),
\end{equation}
and the \emph{conditional} of $\va$ given $\vb$ is Gaussian with
\begin{align}
    \E[\va \mid \vb] &= \vm_a + \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}(\vb - \vm_b),
        \label{eq:condmean}\\
    \cov(\va \mid \vb) &= \Sigma_{aa} - \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\Sigma_{ba}.
        \label{eq:condcov}
\end{align}
\end{funfact}

\begin{proof}
The marginal is immediate: $\va = [\,I \;\; 0\,]\begin{psmallmatrix}\va\\\vb\end{psmallmatrix}$
is an affine map of a Gaussian, so \cref{fact:affine} gives
$\va \sim \Normal(\vm_a, \Sigma_{aa})$.

For the conditional, subtract off the part of $\va$ that $\vb$ can explain and
define the \emph{residual}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual}
    \vr = (\va - \vm_a) - \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}(\vb - \vm_b).
\end{equation}
Being an affine function of the joint Gaussian \eqref{eq:joint}, $\vr$ is Gaussian
with mean $\mathbf 0$. It is \emph{uncorrelated} with $\vb$,
\begin{equation}
    \cov(\vr, \vb)
        = \Sigma_{ab} - \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\Sigma_{bb}
        = 0,
\end{equation}
and for jointly Gaussian vectors uncorrelated means independent. Its covariance is
\begin{equation}
    \cov(\vr)
        = \Sigma_{aa}
          - 2\,\Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\Sigma_{ba}
          + \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\Sigma_{bb}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\Sigma_{ba}
        = \Sigma_{aa} - \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\Sigma_{ba}.
\end{equation}
Rearranging \eqref{eq:residual},
$\va = \vm_a + \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}(\vb - \vm_b) + \vr$. Conditioning on
$\vb$ freezes the middle term, while $\vr$---independent of $\vb$---keeps its
distribution. This gives the mean \eqref{eq:condmean} and covariance
\eqref{eq:condcov}.
\end{proof}

Equation \eqref{eq:marginal} says marginalizing is \emph{free}---ignore the
blocks you do not care about. The conditional mean \eqref{eq:condmean} is the
prior mean $\vm_a$ nudged by the observed discrepancy $\vb - \vm_b$, and the
conditional covariance \eqref{eq:condcov} (a \emph{Schur complement}) is smaller
than $\Sigma_{aa}$---observing $\vb$ can only reduce our uncertainty about
$\va$.

\emph{One dimension.} For two correlated scalars with correlation $\rho$, the
conditional mean $\E[a \mid b] = \mu_a + \frac{\sigma_{ab}}{\sigma_b^2}(b -
\mu_b)$ is exactly the least-squares regression line, and the conditional
variance $\sigma_a^2 - \sigma_{ab}^2/\sigma_b^2 = \sigma_a^2(1 - \rho^2)$ shrinks
by the factor $1 - \rho^2$: the more correlated $b$ is with $a$, the more it
tells us.

\section{Probabilistic PCA}\label{sec:ppca}

Probabilistic principal components analysis (PPCA) \parencite{Tipping1999-cv} is an
alternative interpretation of PCA. The vector observations are treated as
low-dimensional latent cause seen through a linear map, corrupted by isotropic
noise. Assume the data has been centered (the sample mean subtracted), so all
means are zero. The \emph{generative model} is
\begin{genmodel}
\begin{align}
    \vz &\sim \Normal(\mathbf 0, I_d), \label{eq:ppca-latent}\\
    \vy &= C\vz + \veta, \qquad \veta \sim \Normal(\mathbf 0, \sigma^2 I_m),
        \label{eq:ppca-obs}
\end{align}
\end{genmodel}
with $\vz$ and $\veta$ independent. The latent $\vz \in \reals^d$ are
latent factors; the loading matrix $C \in \reals^{m\times d}$ maps them into the
$m$ observed dimensions; and every observed coordinate gets the \emph{same}
noise variance $\sigma^2$---the ``isotropic'' assumption that distinguishes
PPCA from factor analysis later.

Writing out the shapes makes the \textit{dimensionality reduction} visible. The model
only requires $m \geq d$, but the regime of interest is $d \ll m$: a handful of
latent factors driving many observed coordinates. The loading $C$ is then tall
and thin,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppca-shapes}
    \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_m \end{bmatrix}}_{m \times 1}
    =
    \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
        C_{11} & \cdots & C_{1d} \\
        C_{21} & \cdots & C_{2d} \\
        \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        C_{m1} & \cdots & C_{md}
    \end{bmatrix}}_{m \times d}
    \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} z_1 \\ \vdots \\ z_d \end{bmatrix}}_{d \times 1}
    +
    \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \eta_1 \\ \eta_2 \\ \vdots \\ \eta_m \end{bmatrix}}_{m \times 1} .
\end{equation}
Reading $C$ by its columns $C = [\,\vc_1 \;\cdots\; \vc_d\,]$, the model is
$\vy = \sum_{k=1}^d z_k \vc_k + \veta$: each latent coordinate $z_k$ turns on a
loading direction $\vc_k \in \reals^m$, and the observation is their weighted sum
plus noise.

\paragraph{What does $\vy$ look like?}
Because $\vy$ is a linear image of the Gaussian $\vz$ plus independent Gaussian
noise, our fun facts hand us its distribution immediately. It is zero-mean, and
combining the affine map (\cref{fact:affine}) on $C\vz$ with the independent sum
(\cref{fact:sum}) for the noise,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppca-marginal}
    \vy \sim \Normal\!\left(\mathbf 0,\; \underbrace{C C\trp + \sigma^2 I_m}_{\displaystyle \Sigma_{\vy}}\right).
\end{equation}
So PPCA models the data covariance as a low-rank part $C C\trp$ plus an isotropic
floor $\sigma^2 I_m$. Writing $C = [\,\vc_1\ \cdots\ \vc_d\,]$ through its columns
$\vc_k \in \reals^m$, the low-rank part is a sum of $d$ rank-one outer
products---the tall $m \times d$ block $C$ times the wide $d \times m$ block
$C\trp$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppca-lowrank}
    C C\trp \quad=\quad
    \framebox[0.9cm]{\raisebox{0pt}[1.0cm][1.0cm]{$C$}}
    \;\;
    \framebox[1.8cm]{\raisebox{0mm}[4.5mm][2.5mm]{$C\trp$}}
    \quad=\quad \sum_{k=1}^d \vc_k \vc_k\trp ,
\end{equation}
one bump of shared variance per latent factor. Each $\vc_k \vc_k\trp$ is rank
one, and summing the $d$ of them gives a rank-$d$ matrix ($\rank C C\trp = d$
when the loading columns are linearly independent, and at most $d$ in general).
The observation covariance is these few strong directions of shared variation
riding on a uniform background of noise $\sigma^2 I_m$.

\subsection{Inference: the posterior over the latent}

Given an observation $\vy$, what latent factors likely produced it? We want
$p(\vz \mid \vy)$. The pair $(\vz, \vy)$ is jointly Gaussian---both are linear
functions of the independent Gaussians $\vz$ and $\veta$---so \cref{fact:cond}
applies once we have the covariance blocks. From \eqref{eq:ppca-latent} and
\eqref{eq:ppca-obs},
\begin{equation}
    \cov(\vz) = I_d, \qquad
    \cov(\vy) = C C\trp + \sigma^2 I_m, \qquad
    \cov(\vz, \vy) = \E[\vz(C\vz + \veta)\trp] = C\trp .
\end{equation}
Feeding these into the conditioning formula (\cref{fact:cond}, with $\va = \vz$
and $\vb = \vy$),
\begin{align}
    \E[\vz \mid \vy] &= C\trp(C C\trp + \sigma^2 I_m)^{-1}\vy, \label{eq:ppca-postmean-m}\\
    \cov(\vz \mid \vy) &= I_d - C\trp(C C\trp + \sigma^2 I_m)^{-1} C. \label{eq:ppca-postcov-m}
\end{align}
These invert an $m \times m$ matrix, but with $d \ll m$ we would rather invert
something $d \times d$. The matrix inversion lemma (``push-through identity''),
$(C C\trp + \sigma^2 I_m)^{-1} C = C\,(C\trp C + \sigma^2 I_d)^{-1}$, lets us do
exactly that. Defining the $d \times d$ matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppca-M}
    M = C\trp C + \sigma^2 I_d,
\end{equation}
the posterior collapses to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppca-posterior}
    p(\vz \mid \vy) = \Normal\!\left(M^{-1} C\trp \vy,\; \sigma^2 M^{-1}\right).
\end{equation}
(The covariance follows from
$I_d - C\trp(CC\trp+\sigma^2 I_m)^{-1}C = I_d - M^{-1} C\trp C = I_d - M^{-1}(M - \sigma^2 I_d) = \sigma^2 M^{-1}$,
using $C\trp C = M - \sigma^2 I_d$.) Now the only inverse is the small $d \times d$
matrix $M$. The posterior mean is a linear read-out of $\vy$---not the naive
$C\trp\vy$, but shrunk through $M^{-1}$; as $\sigma^2 \to 0$ it approaches the
least-squares projection $(C\trp C)^{-1}C\trp\vy$ onto the latent subspace.

\begin{exercise}
Using the posterior \eqref{eq:ppca-posterior}, show that as $\sigma^2 \to 0$ the
posterior mean $M^{-1}C\trp\vy$ approaches the least-squares projection
$(C\trp C)^{-1}C\trp\vy$ (assume $C$ has full column rank).
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
As $\sigma^2 \to 0$, $M = C\trp C + \sigma^2 I_d \to C\trp C$, which is
invertible when $C$ has full column rank; hence
$M^{-1}C\trp\vy \to (C\trp C)^{-1}C\trp\vy$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[optional stretch]
Show observing $\vy$ never increases uncertainty about $\vz$: the posterior
covariance $\sigma^2 M^{-1}$ satisfies $I_d - \sigma^2 M^{-1} \succeq 0$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
$I_d - \sigma^2 M^{-1} = M^{-1}(M - \sigma^2 I_d) = M^{-1} C\trp C$. Since $M$ and
$C\trp C$ share eigenvectors ($M = C\trp C + \sigma^2 I_d$), the product is
symmetric with nonnegative eigenvalues, so $M^{-1}C\trp C \succeq 0$.
\end{solution}

\subsection{Learning: maximum likelihood}

The parameters $C$ and $\sigma^2$ are fit by maximizing the likelihood of the
data $\{\vy_n\}_{n=1}^N$ under the marginal \eqref{eq:ppca-marginal}. Each
$\vy_n \sim \Normal(\mathbf 0, \Sigma_{\vy})$ with $\Sigma_{\vy} = C C\trp +
\sigma^2 I_m$, so the log-likelihood is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppca-loglik}
    \ell(C, \sigma^2)
      = -\frac{N}{2}\Big[\,m \ln(2\pi) + \ln\det\Sigma_{\vy}
        + \tr\!\big(\Sigma_{\vy}^{-1} S\big)\Big],
    \qquad
    S = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N \vy_n \vy_n\trp .
\end{equation}
It touches the data only through the sample covariance $S$---the very statistic
classical PCA diagonalizes.

Recall the \emph{eigendecomposition} of a symmetric positive-semidefinite matrix:
$S$ can be written $S = U \Lambda U\trp$, where $U = [\,\vu_1\ \cdots\ \vu_m\,]$
is orthogonal (its columns $\vu_i$ are orthonormal \emph{eigenvectors}, the
principal directions) and $\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m)$ holds
the \emph{eigenvalues}, ordered $\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_m \geq 0$.
Each $\lambda_i$ is the variance of the data along direction $\vu_i$. Collect the
top $d$ into $U_d = [\,\vu_1\ \cdots\ \vu_d\,]$ (the $d$ highest-variance
directions) and $\Lambda_d = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d)$; this
$U_d$ is exactly what PCA returns.

Setting $\partial\ell/\partial C = 0$ has, remarkably, a closed-form global
maximum \parencite{Tipping1999-cv}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppca-ML}
    C_{\mathrm{ML}} = U_d\,(\Lambda_d - \sigma^2 I_d)^{1/2}\,R,
\end{equation}
with $U_d$ and $\Lambda_d$ the top-$d$ eigenvectors and eigenvalues of $S$ just
defined, and $R$ any $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix. That $R$ is the rotation ambiguity we
already saw: $C \mapsto CR$ leaves $C C\trp$, hence the likelihood, unchanged.
The noise variance is the average of the $m-d$ \emph{discarded} eigenvalues (so
the reduction assumes $d < m$),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppca-sigmaML}
    \sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac{1}{m - d}\sum_{j = d+1}^{m} \lambda_j ,
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the leftover variance PPCA cannot explain, spread uniformly. So PPCA
recovers the principal subspace: the columns of $C_{\mathrm{ML}}$ span the
top-$d$ eigenvectors of $S$---exactly PCA's directions---with the residual
variance folded into $\sigma^2$. As $\sigma^2 \to 0$ the loadings collapse onto
the scaled principal axes and PPCA reduces to classical PCA.

\paragraph{PPCA is PCA, made probabilistic.}
Both return the \emph{same subspace}. The PPCA loading \eqref{eq:ppca-ML} is $U_d$
times an invertible $d \times d$ matrix (given a spectral gap $\lambda_d >
\sigma^2$), and right-multiplication never changes the column space, so
$\col(C_{\mathrm{ML}}) = \col(U_d)$: the loadings span the principal subspace
exactly. They differ only in coordinates---PCA scales axis $k$ by
$\sqrt{\lambda_k}$, PPCA by $\sqrt{\lambda_k - \sigma^2}$ (stripping the noise
floor), orientation free through $R$. As $\sigma^2 \to 0$ they coincide: the
reconstruction $C\,\E[\vz \mid \vy] \to U_d U_d\trp \vy$ is PCA's orthogonal
projection onto $\col(U_d)$ (the posterior mean itself is the whitened score
$\Lambda_d^{-1/2} U_d\trp \vy$). In short, PPCA's maximum-likelihood fit \emph{is}
PCA, recast as inference in a linear-Gaussian model.

\paragraph{Two caveats, and the road to factor analysis.}
The equivalence cuts both ways, with a caveat in each direction. First, PCA is
\emph{more general} than its probabilistic reading: as a variance-maximizing
(equivalently, best least-squares subspace) procedure it can be run on data that
are neither Gaussian nor generated by a linear model---PPCA is one probabilistic
interpretation, not a prerequisite. Second, and pulling the other way, PPCA's
isotropic noise $\sigma^2 I_m$ is a strong assumption: every observed coordinate is
forced to carry the \emph{same} private noise variance. Real measurements rarely
oblige---different sensors, neurons, or features have different noise scales.
Relaxing $\sigma^2 I_m$ to a general diagonal noise covariance is exactly factor
analysis, the subject of the next section.

\section{Factor analysis}\label{sec:fa}

Factor analysis (FA) keeps everything about PPCA except the noise: rather than one
shared variance $\sigma^2$, every observed coordinate gets its own $\sigma_i^2$.
The generative model is
\begin{genmodel}
\begin{align}
    \vz &\sim \Normal(\mathbf 0, I_d), \qquad \vy = C\vz + \veta, \label{eq:fa-latent}\\
    \veta &\sim \Normal(\mathbf 0,\ \Sigma_{\veta}), \qquad
    \Sigma_{\veta} = \diag(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_m^2). \label{eq:fa-obs}
\end{align}
\end{genmodel}
Here $\vz$ and $\veta$ are independent and the noise is zero-mean Gaussian with a
\emph{diagonal} covariance,
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{\veta} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \sigma_1^2 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
        0 & \sigma_2^2 & \cdots & 0 \\
        \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        0 & 0 & \cdots & \sigma_m^2
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The zeros off the diagonal say the noise in different coordinates is
uncorrelated; the diagonal entries $\sigma_i^2$ let each coordinate be exactly as
noisy as it needs to be. In the classical vocabulary the latent $\vz$ are the
\emph{common factors} (shared across all coordinates), the loading columns
$\vc_k$ say how strongly each factor drives each coordinate, and $\veta$ is the
\emph{unique} (or specific) noise, private to each coordinate.

The marginal follows from the same two fun facts,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fa-marginal}
    \vy \sim \Normal\!\left(\mathbf 0,\; C C\trp + \Sigma_{\veta}\right).
\end{equation}
The only change from PPCA's \eqref{eq:ppca-marginal} is $\sigma^2 I_m \mapsto
\Sigma_{\veta}$: the isotropic noise floor becomes a diagonal one, with PPCA the
special case $\sigma_1^2 = \cdots = \sigma_m^2$. The shared signal is still the
low-rank $C C\trp$; what FA buys is the freedom to soak up wildly different
per-coordinate noise levels instead of forcing them all equal.

\subsection{Inference: the posterior over the latent}

Inference mirrors PPCA exactly, with $\Sigma_{\veta}$ wherever $\sigma^2 I_m$
stood. The pair $(\vz, \vy)$ is jointly Gaussian with the same blocks as before,
now
\begin{equation}
    \cov(\vz) = I_d, \qquad
    \cov(\vy) = C C\trp + \Sigma_{\veta}, \qquad
    \cov(\vz, \vy) = C\trp,
\end{equation}
so \cref{fact:cond} gives
\begin{equation}
    \E[\vz \mid \vy] = C\trp(C C\trp + \Sigma_{\veta})^{-1}\vy, \qquad
    \cov(\vz \mid \vy) = I_d - C\trp(C C\trp + \Sigma_{\veta})^{-1} C.
\end{equation}
Again we would rather invert a $d \times d$ matrix. The matrix inversion lemma,
now with the diagonal $\Sigma_{\veta}$, does it: defining
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fa-M}
    M = I_d + C\trp \Sigma_{\veta}^{-1} C \qquad (d \times d),
\end{equation}
the posterior collapses to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fa-posterior}
    p(\vz \mid \vy) = \Normal\!\left(M^{-1} C\trp \Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}\vy,\; M^{-1}\right).
\end{equation}
(As before the covariance follows from
$I_d - C\trp(CC\trp+\Sigma_{\veta})^{-1}C = I_d - M^{-1}C\trp\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}C
= I_d - M^{-1}(M - I_d) = M^{-1}$, using $C\trp\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}C = M - I_d$.)
The one genuinely new ingredient is the inverse noise covariance
$\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1} = \diag(1/\sigma_1^2, \dots, 1/\sigma_m^2)$, trivial because
$\Sigma_{\veta}$ is diagonal. The data enters the posterior mean as
$C\trp \Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}\vy$: each coordinate is \emph{precision-weighted} by
$1/\sigma_i^2$ before being read into the latent space, so noisy coordinates
(large $\sigma_i^2$) are automatically discounted---the payoff of letting the
noise vary per dimension. Setting all $\sigma_i^2 = \sigma^2$ collapses $M$ and
\eqref{eq:fa-posterior} back to the PPCA posterior \eqref{eq:ppca-posterior}.

\subsection{Learning: EM, not eigenvectors}

The parameters $C$ and $\Sigma_{\veta}$ are again fit by maximizing the marginal
likelihood, whose log has the same shape as PPCA's \eqref{eq:ppca-loglik} with
$\Sigma_{\vy} = C C\trp + \Sigma_{\veta}$. But here the pleasant closed form
disappears: the diagonal constraint on $\Sigma_{\veta}$ couples it with $C$ and
breaks the eigendecomposition that solved PPCA. Factor analysis has \emph{no
closed-form maximum likelihood estimate}; it must be optimized iteratively.

The standard tool is \emph{expectation--maximization} (EM), which alternates
between the inference we just did and a parameter update:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{E-step.} With the current $C, \Sigma_{\veta}$, compute the
    posterior over each latent from \eqref{eq:fa-posterior}, $q(\vz_n) =
    \Normal(\vmu_n, M^{-1})$ with $\vmu_n = M^{-1}C\trp\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}\vy_n$,
    and collect the sufficient statistics
    \begin{equation}
        \E[\vz_n] = \vmu_n, \qquad
        \E[\vz_n\vz_n\trp] = M^{-1} + \vmu_n\vmu_n\trp .
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{M-step.} Holding those fixed, maximize the expected
    complete-data log-likelihood over $C$ and $\Sigma_{\veta}$; both updates are
    closed-form,
    \begin{align}
        C^{\text{new}} &= \Big(\textstyle\sum_n \vy_n \E[\vz_n]\trp\Big)
                          \Big(\textstyle\sum_n \E[\vz_n\vz_n\trp]\Big)^{-1},
                          \label{eq:fa-Mstep-C}\\
        \Sigma_{\veta}^{\text{new}} &= \diag\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_n
            \big(\vy_n\vy_n\trp - C^{\text{new}}\E[\vz_n]\vy_n\trp\big)\right),
            \label{eq:fa-Mstep-S}
    \end{align}
    where $\diag(\cdot)$ keeps only the diagonal, enforcing the noise model.
\end{itemize}
Each round cannot decrease the likelihood, so EM climbs monotonically---but only
to a \emph{local} maximum. Like any local method it carries no guarantee of
reaching the global optimum, and different initializations can converge to
different solutions \parencite{Bishop2006-to}; in practice one restarts from
several initializations and keeps the best.

\section{The factor rotation problem}\label{sec:rotation}

There is a deeper ambiguity, one the data can never resolve. Take any orthogonal
$d \times d$ matrix $R$ (so $R R\trp = R\trp R = I_d$) and replace the loading and
latent by
\begin{equation}
    C \mapsto CR, \qquad \vz \mapsto R\trp \vz .
\end{equation}
The observation is untouched, $CR\,(R\trp\vz) = C\vz$, and the rotated latent is
still standard, $R\trp\vz \sim \Normal(\mathbf 0,\, R\trp I_d R) = \Normal(\mathbf
0, I_d)$. The marginal covariance is invariant too,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rotation-invariance}
    (CR)(CR)\trp = C\,R R\trp\,C\trp = C C\trp,
\end{equation}
so the likelihood is \emph{exactly the same} for $C$ and $CR$. The data cannot
tell the two apart: only the subspace $\col(C)$ and the noise are identified, not
the individual loading columns---the ``factors'' themselves.

Three concrete symmetries live inside this freedom, because permutation and
sign-flip matrices are themselves orthogonal:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Permutation.} Reordering the columns of $C$ (relabeling the
    factors) is a rotation by a permutation matrix---the factors carry no
    intrinsic order.
    \item \emph{Sign flip.} Negating a column, $\vc_k \mapsto -\vc_k$, is a
    rotation by $\diag(\pm 1)$---each factor's sign is arbitrary.
    \item \emph{Continuous rotation.} Beyond these discrete moves, a general
    orthogonal $R$ blends the factors into new linear combinations of one
    another.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Isn't PCA immune?} No. We already met this rotation as the free $R$ in
the PPCA maximum likelihood \eqref{eq:ppca-ML}. PCA and PPCA merely \emph{look}
unique because they adopt a convention---take the loading columns orthogonal and
ordered by decreasing variance (the eigenvectors of $S$). Ordering removes the
permutation freedom, and demanding orthogonal axes with \emph{distinct}
eigenvalues removes the continuous rotation, but the \emph{sign flips survive}:
each principal component is pinned only up to $\pm$. And the instant two
eigenvalues coincide, the rotation freedom within that eigenspace returns in
full. Factor analysis adopts no ordering-or-orthogonality convention at all, so
the entire rotation group is left open---which is why the ambiguity is felt most
sharply there.

\paragraph{A bug turned into a feature.} Since rotation leaves the fit untouched
\eqref{eq:rotation-invariance}, one can \emph{choose} the rotation that makes the
loadings interpretable---ideally each variable loading on only a few factors.
This is \emph{factor rotation} (e.g.\ \emph{varimax}, which drives each column
toward a ``simple structure''); it changes the description, never the fit. The
lesson is a cautionary one: an individual factor has no intrinsic meaning.
Defined only up to rotation, its meaning must come from \emph{outside} the
model---either by fixing a rotation convention or by anchoring the factors to
known covariates (a stimulus, behavior, or condition) through post-hoc
regression. ``What factor $k$ represents'' is always a statement about that
external choice, not about the latent model alone.

\section{Units and scale: PCA versus FA}\label{sec:scale}

A second difference between the two models is subtler than the noise floor but
just as consequential: what happens when you change the \emph{units} of the
observed coordinates. Rescale each coordinate by its own factor,
$\vy \mapsto D\vy$ with $D = \diag(a_1, \dots, a_m)$ (think millimeters to
centimeters, or a different gain on each sensor). Under the FA model this is
\emph{again} an FA model:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fa-rescale}
    D\vy = (DC)\vz + D\veta, \qquad
    D\veta \sim \Normal\!\big(\mathbf 0,\ D\Sigma_{\veta}D\big)
          = \Normal\!\big(\mathbf 0,\ \diag(a_1^2\sigma_1^2, \dots, a_m^2\sigma_m^2)\big).
\end{equation}
The rescaled noise covariance is still diagonal, so the rescaled data obeys
factor analysis with loading $DC$ and noise $a_i^2\sigma_i^2$---and the latent
factors $\vz$ are \emph{unchanged}. Factor analysis is equivariant to
per-coordinate rescaling: it does not care what units each coordinate is measured
in.

PCA and PPCA enjoy no such property. The isotropic floor is not preserved,
\begin{equation}
    D(\sigma^2 I_m)D = \sigma^2 D^2 = \diag(\sigma^2 a_1^2, \dots, \sigma^2 a_m^2),
\end{equation}
which is no longer isotropic unless all $a_i$ are equal---a rescaled PPCA model
is an FA model, not a PPCA one. Equivalently, PCA diagonalizes $S$, and rescaling
sends $S \mapsto DSD$, whose eigenvectors bear no simple relation to those of $S$.
Change the units of your variables and PCA hands you different principal axes.
This is exactly why PCA is usually run on \emph{standardized} (z-scored) data---to
blunt its dependence on arbitrary units---whereas factor analysis needs no such
preprocessing.

The practical upshot: when the observed coordinates are commensurable (same
units, comparable scales), PCA and PPCA are natural. When they are not (different
sensors, different neurons, heterogeneous features), factor analysis---by soaking
each coordinate's scale into its own $\sigma_i^2$---is the better-posed model.

\section{Non-Gaussian observations: Poisson FA}\label{sec:expfam}

So far the observation was a linear-Gaussian image of the latent: $\vy = C\vz +
\veta$ with Gaussian noise. That is fine for real-valued measurements, but it
fails the moment the data are counts. Spike trains, the currency of the neural
notebooks, are non-negative integers---a neuron in a time bin fires $0,1,2,\dots$ times.
A Gaussian read-out puts probability mass on negative and
fractional $y$, which no count can take. We need an emission that lives on the
integers and stays non-negative.
We keep the linear latent-to-parameter map $C\vz + \vb$, swap only the Gaussian emission for a
Poisson one, and watch which pieces of the linear-Gaussian machinery survive.

Technically, any simple point process can be fully characterized by its conditional
intensity function \parencite{Daley1988-te}, and further it can be approximated as a
Poisson distribution given small enough bin size \parencite{Kass2014-st}.

\subsection{Poisson distribution}

A \emph{Poisson} random variable $y \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ counts events that
occur independently at a constant expected rate over a fixed interval. Its
probability mass function has a single parameter $\mu > 0$, the expected count,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson-pmf}
    P(y = k) = \frac{\mu^k\, e^{-\mu}}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots
\end{equation}

\begin{funfact}[Poisson mean equals variance]\label{fact:poisson-moments}
For $y \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson-moments}
    \E[y] = \var(y) = \mu .
\end{equation}
The one parameter $\mu$ sets both the location and the spread; the Fano factor
$\var(y)/\E[y]$ is exactly $1$. There is no separate noise scale to choose---the
crux of \cref{ex:poisson-noise}.
\end{funfact}

This is also why, for count data, a factor-analysis mindset is better posed than
PCA. Because $\var(y_i) = \mu_i$ scales with each neuron's rate, the
observation noise is inherently \emph{heteroscedastic}: a high-rate neuron is
noisier, in absolute terms, than a low-rate one. PCA's isotropic $\sigma^2 I_m$
(\cref{sec:scale}) cannot represent that, whereas factor analysis's
per-coordinate $\sigma_i^2$ can. The Poisson emission below sidesteps the choice
entirely by tying the noise to the rate directly.
This is why often PCA on neural data collapses to capture a single neuron with the highest firing rate.

\subsection{The model}

The latent keeps its standard-Gaussian prior, exactly as in PPCA and factor
analysis. The affine image $C\vz + \vb$ is now read as a vector of \emph{log
firing rates}---the ``natural'' parameter of the emission---exponentiated to a
strictly positive rate, and over a bin of width $\Delta$ the observed counts are
conditionally independent Poisson,
\begin{genmodel}
\begin{align}
    \vz &\sim \Normal(\mathbf 0, I_d), \label{eq:expfam-latent}\\
    \bm{\theta} &= C\vz + \vb, \qquad \bm{\lambda} = \exp(\bm{\theta}) = \exp(C\vz + \vb)
        \quad\text{(elementwise)}, \label{eq:expfam-rate}\\
    y_i \mid \vz &\sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\Delta\,\lambda_i), \qquad i = 1, \dots, m . \label{eq:expfam-obs}
\end{align}
\end{genmodel}
The log link $\lambda_i = e^{\theta_i}$ guarantees $\lambda_i > 0$ no matter what
the latent does.
Elementwise this is a \emph{Poisson generalized linear model} (GLM) with a latent
regressor: row $\vc_i\trp$ of $C$ is neuron $i$'s tuning vector, $b_i$ its
baseline log-rate, and a step in the latent moves every neuron's rate
\emph{multiplicatively} (an additive shift in log-rate). The shapes are the PPCA
shapes of \eqref{eq:ppca-shapes} unchanged---$C \in \reals^{m\times d}$ tall and
thin, $\vz \in \reals^d$---with a new bias $\vb \in \reals^m$ and the linear
output reinterpreted as a log-rate rather than a mean. Throughout we take
$\Delta = \mathrm{d}t$ the bin width; setting $\Delta = 1$ absorbs it into $\vb$.

\subsection{The log-likelihood}

The Poisson pmf for one neuron is $p(y_i \mid \vz) = (\Delta\lambda_i)^{y_i}
e^{-\Delta\lambda_i}/y_i!$. Taking logs and using $\log\lambda_i = \theta_i =
\vc_i\trp\vz + b_i$, then summing over the conditionally independent neurons,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expfam-loglik}
    \log p(\vy \mid \vz) = \sum_{i=1}^m \Big[\, y_i\,(\vc_i\trp\vz + b_i)
        - \Delta\, e^{\vc_i\trp\vz + b_i} \,\Big] + \mathrm{const}(\vy, \Delta) .
\end{equation}
Three pieces: a \emph{linear} term $y_i\,\theta_i$ (data times natural
parameter), a \emph{convex penalty} $\Delta e^{\theta_i}$ (the expected count),
and a data-only constant $\log(y_i!) - y_i\log\Delta$ that never touches $\vz$.
Adding the log-prior gives the log-joint, the object both inference and learning
optimize,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expfam-logjoint}
    \log p(\vz, \vy) = \sum_{i=1}^m \Big[\, y_i(\vc_i\trp\vz + b_i)
        - \Delta e^{\vc_i\trp\vz + b_i} \,\Big] - \tfrac12 \vz\trp\vz + \mathrm{const} .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Exponential family: one machine, many emissions}

Poisson and Gaussian are not rivals; they are two members of the same
\emph{exponential family}. Each writes the per-coordinate likelihood as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expfam-form}
    p(y_i \mid \vz) = h(y_i)\,\exp\!\big(\theta_i\, y_i - A(\theta_i)\big),
    \qquad \theta_i = \vc_i\trp\vz + b_i,
\end{equation}
with $\theta_i$ the natural parameter (always the affine image $C\vz + \vb$) and
$A(\cdot)$ the \emph{log-partition} function. The log-partition carries the
moments,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expfam-moments}
    \E[y_i \mid \vz] = A'(\theta_i), \qquad \var(y_i \mid \vz) = A''(\theta_i) .
\end{equation}
For the Poisson $A(\theta) = e^{\theta}$, so $\E[y_i\mid\vz] = \var(y_i\mid\vz) =
\lambda_i$ (mean equals variance---the Fano factor is $1$). For a Gaussian with
identity link $A(\theta) = \theta^2/2$, so the mean is $\theta$ and the variance
is constant---recovering the linear-Gaussian factor-analysis emission of
\cref{sec:fa}. The message: PPCA and FA are the \emph{Gaussian special case};
$C\vz + \vb$ is always the natural-parameter map, and only $A(\cdot)$ changes when
the data change. Applying this generalization to the latent linear model turns
PCA and factor analysis into \emph{exponential-family} PCA
\parencite{Collins2001-ef,Ghahramani2009-of}; for neural spike trains the Poisson
case yields the Poisson linear dynamical system and related low-dimensional
latent-variable models \parencite{Macke2011-ep,Yu2009-qp,Pfau2013-oi,Buesing2013-ce}.

\subsection{What breaks: no conjugacy}

In the linear-Gaussian world \cref{fact:cond} handed us an exact Gaussian
posterior $p(\vz\mid\vy)$ and a closed-form marginal $p(\vy)$. With a Poisson
emission both vanish. The posterior $p(\vz\mid\vy) \propto p(\vy\mid\vz)\,p(\vz)$
is no longer Gaussian---the likelihood \eqref{eq:expfam-loglik} is not a quadratic
in $\vz$---and the marginal $p(\vy) = \int p(\vy\mid\vz)\,p(\vz)\,\mathrm{d}\vz$
has no closed form. Bluntly: the conditioning fun fact no longer applies. There
is no $M$ matrix, no closed-form posterior, no eigen-solution. We must
approximate.

\subsection{What survives: log-concavity, MAP, Laplace}

Not all structure is lost. With the exponential link the log-joint
\eqref{eq:expfam-logjoint} is \emph{concave} in $\vz$. Its gradient is a
spikes-minus-expected-spikes error, back-projected through $C$ and balanced
against the prior pull,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expfam-grad}
    \nabla_{\vz}\log p(\vz, \vy) = C\trp(\vy - \Delta\bm{\lambda}) - \vz ,
\end{equation}
and its Hessian is negative definite everywhere,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expfam-hess}
    \nabla^2_{\vz}\log p(\vz, \vy) = -\big(C\trp \diag(\Delta\bm{\lambda})\,C + I_d\big) \prec 0 .
\end{equation}
So the log-joint has a \emph{unique} maximizer, the MAP estimate $\hat{\vz} =
\arg\max_{\vz}\log p(\vz,\vy)$, found by Newton's method---equivalently
iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS), the standard GLM solver. Expanding
the log-joint to second order at $\hat{\vz}$ gives the \emph{Laplace
approximation} to the posterior,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expfam-laplace}
    p(\vz \mid \vy) \approx \Normal\!\Big(\hat{\vz},\;
        \big(C\trp \diag(\Delta\hat{\bm{\lambda}})\,C + I_d\big)^{-1}\Big),
    \qquad \hat{\bm{\lambda}} = \exp(C\hat{\vz} + \vb) .
\end{equation}
Compare the posterior precision with factor analysis's constant $M = I_d + C\trp
\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1} C$ of \eqref{eq:fa-M}. The shape is identical---prior
precision $I_d$ plus $C\trp(\text{data precision})\,C$---but the Gaussian noise
precision $\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}$ is replaced by the \emph{state-dependent}
$\diag(\Delta\bm{\lambda})$, evaluated at $\hat{\vz}$. That the data precision now
moves with the latent is the single genuinely new phenomenon; everything else is
the FA posterior with a rate-dependent weighting.

\subsection{How much does the population encode? Fisher information and SNR}

The matrix multiplying the loading in \eqref{eq:expfam-laplace},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expfam-fisher}
    \mathcal{I}(\vz) = C\trp \diag\!\big(\Delta\bm{\lambda}(\vz)\big)\, C
                     = \sum_{i=1}^m \Delta\lambda_i\, \vc_i \vc_i\trp ,
\end{equation}
is the \emph{Fisher information} that the population's spike counts carry about
the latent $\vz$---the expected curvature of $-\log p(\vy\mid\vz)$. It sets a
Cram\'er--Rao floor: any unbiased estimate obeys $\cov(\hat{\vz}) \succeq
\mathcal{I}(\vz)^{-1}$, and the Laplace posterior covariance
$(\mathcal{I}(\vz)+I_d)^{-1}$ is exactly this floor sharpened by the unit-variance
prior. Read elementwise, each neuron adds $\Delta\lambda_i\,\vc_i\vc_i\trp$:
information grows with firing rate and with the number of neurons, and is shaped
by how the tuning directions $\vc_i$ tile the latent space. A natural
\emph{signal-to-noise ratio} compares the latent's signal power to this
inverse-Fisher noise floor,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expfam-snr}
    \mathrm{SNR}(\vz) = \frac{\tr \cov(\vz)}{\tr \mathcal{I}(\vz)^{-1}} .
\end{equation}
\textcite{Jeon2024b} turn this into a practical upper bound on the SNR of
\emph{inferred} neural latent trajectories---the bound scales with the per-neuron
Fisher information and the observation overdispersion---and show that inference
which exploits temporal regularity (the smoothing of \cref{sec:smoothing})
approaches it. Notebook~\texttt{01} measures this bound for random versus
axis-aligned loadings.

\subsection{Learning}

The parameters $C$ and $\vb$ are fit by maximizing the (now intractable) marginal
likelihood. EM still applies, but its E-step is no longer exact: the closed-form
Gaussian posterior of factor analysis is replaced by an approximate posterior---
the Laplace form \eqref{eq:expfam-laplace}, or a variational one. This is the
doorway to \cref{sec:vi} (variational inference), and, once dynamics are stacked
on the latent, to XFADS (\cref{sec:xfads}): a Poisson read-out riding on
Kalman-style latent dynamics.

\paragraph{Link functions.} The canonical log link $\bm{\lambda} =
\exp(\bm{\theta})$ is analytically clean and guarantees concavity, but $\exp$ can
overflow numerically for large $\theta_i$. A common alternative is the
\emph{softplus},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expfam-softplus}
    \bm{\lambda} = \log\!\big(\mathbf 1 + \exp(C\vz + \vb)\big) \quad\text{(elementwise)},
\end{equation}
still monotone and positive but with gentler, linear tails; it is non-canonical,
so the tidy $A'$/$A''$ moment identities no longer hold exactly. Notebook 00
compares $\exp$ against softplus emissions. The same
exponential-family read-out reappears on top of the latent-dynamics machinery in
the sections that follow.

\begin{exercise}[gradient, Hessian, concavity]
For the Poisson model with log link, differentiate the log-joint
$\log p(\vz,\vy) = \sum_i[\,y_i(\vc_i\trp\vz + b_i) - \Delta e^{\vc_i\trp\vz + b_i}\,]
- \tfrac12\vz\trp\vz$ to show $\nabla_{\vz} = C\trp(\vy - \Delta\bm{\lambda}) - \vz$
and $\nabla^2_{\vz} = -(C\trp\diag(\Delta\bm{\lambda})C + I_d)$. Conclude the
Hessian is negative definite, so the MAP $\hat{\vz}$ is unique.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Since $\partial_{\vz}(\vc_i\trp\vz) = \vc_i$ and $\partial_{\vz}e^{\vc_i\trp\vz +
b_i} = \lambda_i\vc_i$, the gradient sums $y_i\vc_i - \Delta\lambda_i\vc_i =
C\trp(\vy - \Delta\bm{\lambda})$ minus the prior term $\vz$. Differentiating
again, each neuron contributes $-\Delta\lambda_i\vc_i\vc_i\trp$, giving
$-C\trp\diag(\Delta\bm{\lambda})C$, and the prior adds $-I_d$. Since
$\Delta\lambda_i > 0$, $C\trp\diag(\Delta\bm{\lambda})C \succeq 0$ and $I_d \succ
0$, so the Hessian is negative definite and the log-joint is strictly concave;
its stationary point is the unique maximum.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Poisson has no free noise scale]\label{ex:poisson-noise}
Show that for the Poisson emission $\E[y_i\mid\vz] = \var(y_i\mid\vz) =
\Delta\lambda_i$ (Fano factor $1$), and contrast with factor analysis, where the
emission mean $\vc_i\trp\vz$ and noise variance $\sigma_i^2$ are separate free
quantities. What does this say about identifying a separate per-neuron noise level
in the Poisson model?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
For $y\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)$, mean $=$ variance $= \mu$, here $\mu =
\Delta\lambda_i$; equivalently $A(\theta) = e^{\theta}$ gives $A' = A'' = e^{\theta}
= \lambda_i$. Unlike FA's diagonal $\Sigma_{\veta}$, the Poisson observation noise
is fixed by the rate itself---there is no independent $\sigma_i^2$ to fit.
Heterogeneous variability across neurons must come from the rates (through $C,
\vb$) or from a richer emission (e.g.\ negative binomial, which adds an
overdispersion parameter), not from a separable additive noise term.
\end{solution}

\section{The Kalman filter: a latent that moves}\label{sec:kalman}

So far the latent was static---one hidden cause per observation. Let it now
\emph{evolve in time}. The same linear-Gaussian machinery, made sequential, is
the workhorse of latent \emph{dynamics}.

Take the simplest case: one hidden state $z_t \in \reals$ that drifts, watched by
a noisy scalar sensor,
\begin{genmodel}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kf-model}
  z_t = a\,z_{t-1} + e_t, \quad e_t \sim \Normal(0, Q),
  \qquad
  y_t = z_t + v_t, \quad v_t \sim \Normal(0, R),
\end{equation}
\end{genmodel}
with the process noise, sensor noise, and initial state all mutually independent.
Here $Q$ is how unpredictably the state moves and $R$ how badly the sensor
corrupts it; $a=1$ makes $z_t$ a random walk,
and $y_t = z_t + v_t$ is again ``signal plus noise,'' now one scalar at a time.

We track the \emph{belief} $p(z_t \mid y_{1:t})$: all we know about the state
given the readings so far. Every arrow in \eqref{eq:kf-model} is linear-Gaussian,
so the belief stays Gaussian (fun facts \ref{fact:affine}--\ref{fact:cond}) and we
carry only a mean and a variance, $z_t \mid y_{1:t} \sim \Normal(\mu_t, P_t)$. Two
moves per step update them. (We write $\mu_t^-, P_t^-$ for the \emph{predicted}
belief before seeing $y_t$; the accompanying worksheet uses
$\mu_b, P_b$ in its single-measurement warm-up.)

\paragraph{Predict.} Push the belief through the dynamics---an affine map
(\cref{fact:affine}) plus independent noise (\cref{fact:sum}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kf-predict}
  \mu_t^- = a\,\mu_{t-1}, \qquad P_t^- = a^2 P_{t-1} + Q.
\end{equation}
Process noise $Q$ always adds uncertainty; the drift scales the prior spread by
$a^2$ (inflating it when $|a|>1$, shrinking it when $|a|<1$). For the worksheet's
random walk $a=1$, prediction simply grows the variance by $Q$.

\paragraph{Update.} Condition on $y_t$. We hold a Gaussian prior
$z_t \sim \Normal(\mu_t^-, P_t^-)$ and a Gaussian likelihood
$y_t \mid z_t \sim \Normal(z_t, R)$; Bayes multiplies them. The cleanest reading:
\emph{precisions (inverse variances) add}, and the mean is their precision-weighted
average,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kf-bayes}
  \frac{1}{P_t} = \frac{1}{P_t^-} + \frac{1}{R},
  \qquad
  \mu_t = P_t\!\left(\frac{\mu_t^-}{P_t^-} + \frac{y_t}{R}\right).
\end{equation}
Rearranged into the classic \emph{gain} form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kf-update}
  K_t = \frac{P_t^-}{P_t^- + R},
  \qquad
  \mu_t = \mu_t^- + K_t\,(y_t - \mu_t^-),
  \qquad
  P_t = (1 - K_t)\,P_t^-.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The gain is trust.} The innovation $y_t - \mu_t^-$ is the surprise;
the gain $K_t \in [0,1]$ is the fraction of it we believe. Precise sensor ($R$
small) or vague state ($P_t^-$ large) sends $K_t \to 1$---jump to the reading;
noisy sensor or confident state sends $K_t \to 0$---ignore it. Either way the
belief sharpens, $P_t = (1-K_t)P_t^- \le P_t^-$: a measurement can only help. That
is the entire idea; the accompanying worksheet works it on numbers.

\paragraph{Vector form.} Nothing new in higher dimensions. For
$\vz_t = A\vz_{t-1} + \ve_t$ and $\vy_t = C\vz_t + \vv_t$ (noise covariances $Q,
R$),
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \vmu_t^- &= A\vmu_{t-1}, &\quad P_t^- &= A P_{t-1} A\trp + Q, \\
  K_t &= P_t^- C\trp (C P_t^- C\trp + R)^{-1}, &\quad
  \vmu_t &= \vmu_t^- + K_t(\vy_t - C\vmu_t^-),
\end{aligned}
\qquad P_t = (I - K_t C)P_t^-.
\end{equation}
The bracketed factor $S_t = C P_t^- C\trp + R$ is the \emph{innovation covariance}
and $\vd_t = \vy_t - C\vmu_t^-$ the \emph{innovation}, so $K_t = P_t^- C\trp
S_t^{-1}$. Scalars became matrices and $1/(P^-+R)$ became $S_t^{-1}$;
predict-then-update, gain-weighted innovation, shrinking covariance---all
identical. With $C=I$ and diagonal matrices it splits into independent 1D
filters: the worksheet's 2D case, where $S_t = P_t^- + R$. (Heads-up on notation:
that worksheet names its two state coordinates $x$ and $y$, so it cannot reuse
$y$ for the measurement and writes the \emph{observation} as $\vz_t$---the one
place $z$ denotes a measurement rather than the latent, giving $\vd_t = \vz_t -
\vmu_t^-$.)

\paragraph{Least squares, and model mismatch.} As with PCA, one recursion has two
readings. The Bayesian one above is \emph{exact when the model is
linear-Gaussian}: \eqref{eq:kf-update} is the true posterior. But the same
equations are also the \emph{linear minimum-mean-squared-error} (LMMSE)
estimator: given the linear model \eqref{eq:kf-model} and the noise
covariances---whatever the underlying noise distribution---they return the best
estimate of $z_t$ among all \emph{linear} functions of the data. So when
Gaussianity fails the filter degrades gracefully: it is no longer the full
posterior, nor the best nonlinear estimate, but it is still the best linear one.
Bayes gives the clean derivation; least squares explains why the filter is
trusted well outside the world it was derived in---exactly the PCA parallel.

\paragraph{Numerical stability (secondary).} The update $P_t = (I - K_tC)P_t^-$ is
the clearest form but not the most stable: in finite precision it can lose
symmetry or positive-definiteness. Production filters use the equivalent
\emph{Joseph form}
$P_t = (I - K_tC)P_t^-(I - K_tC)\trp + K_t R K_t\trp$, which stays symmetric
positive-\emph{semi}definite, or propagate a Cholesky square root of $P_t$. These change the
arithmetic, not the estimate.

\section{Smoothing and forecasting: using all the data, and running past it}\label{sec:smoothing}

The forward filter of \cref{sec:kalman} reports $p(\vz_t \mid \vy_{1:t})$: the
belief about the state using only the past, up to now. Two other beliefs are just
as natural. \emph{Smoothing} uses the \emph{whole} record, $p(\vz_t \mid
\vy_{1:T})$ with $T \geq t$---the offline, retrospective estimate, and the E-step
of learning. \emph{Forecasting} looks the other way, $p(\vz_{T+h} \mid
\vy_{1:T})$, $h$ steps past the last observation. Both fall straight out of the
same fun facts and the filter quantities already in hand; nothing new to compute
but a backward sweep and a repeated predict. (Notebook 02's \texttt{rts\_smoother}
is exactly this section; ``look how much smoother the samples are'' is the payoff
below.)

\subsection{RTS smoothing: a backward pass}

The forward pass produces, at each $t$, the filtered belief $\vz_t \mid \vy_{1:t}
\sim \Normal(\vmu_t, P_t)$ and the predicted belief $\vz_{t+1} \mid \vy_{1:t} \sim
\Normal(\vmu_{t+1}^-, P_{t+1}^-)$. Introduce the \emph{smoothed} belief $\vz_t
\mid \vy_{1:T} \sim \Normal(\vmu_t^s, P_t^s)$ (superscript $s$ for smoothed,
parallel to the minus for predicted), together with the lagged cross-covariance
$P_{t,t+1}^s = \cov(\vz_t, \vz_{t+1} \mid \vy_{1:T})$. The plan: run the filter
forward once, storing $\vmu_t, P_t, \vmu_t^-, P_t^-$; then sweep $t = T-1, \dots,
1$ backward.

\paragraph{The Markov decoupling.} One structural fact makes a single backward
pass enough: conditioned on $\vz_{t+1}$, the past and future observations are
independent, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sm-markov}
    p(\vz_t \mid \vz_{t+1}, \vy_{1:T}) = p(\vz_t \mid \vz_{t+1}, \vy_{1:t}) .
\end{equation}
Once the next state is known, the future tells us nothing more about $\vz_t$.

\paragraph{The backward conditional.} Build the joint of $(\vz_t, \vz_{t+1})$
given $\vy_{1:t}$. The marginals are the filtered $\Normal(\vmu_t, P_t)$ and the
predicted $\Normal(\vmu_{t+1}^-, P_{t+1}^-)$; the cross-block comes from
$\vz_{t+1} = A\vz_t + \ve_{t+1}$, giving $\cov(\vz_t, \vz_{t+1} \mid \vy_{1:t}) =
P_t A\trp$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sm-joint}
    \begin{pmatrix} \vz_t \\ \vz_{t+1} \end{pmatrix} \Big|\, \vy_{1:t}
    \sim \Normal\!\left(
        \begin{pmatrix} \vmu_t \\ \vmu_{t+1}^- \end{pmatrix},\;
        \begin{pmatrix} P_t & P_t A\trp \\ A P_t & P_{t+1}^- \end{pmatrix}
    \right) .
\end{equation}
Conditioning $\vz_t$ on $\vz_{t+1}$ with \cref{fact:cond}, and defining the
\emph{smoother gain}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sm-gain}
    J_t = P_t A\trp (P_{t+1}^-)^{-1},
\end{equation}
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sm-backcond}
    \E[\vz_t \mid \vz_{t+1}, \vy_{1:t}] = \vmu_t + J_t(\vz_{t+1} - \vmu_{t+1}^-),
    \qquad
    \cov(\vz_t \mid \vz_{t+1}, \vy_{1:t}) = P_t - J_t P_{t+1}^- J_t\trp .
\end{equation}
The gain $J_t$ is the backward analogue of the Kalman gain $K_t$ of
\eqref{eq:kf-update}---how much of the downstream news to trust---and is a
different object (do not confuse it with the information matrix notebook 02 calls
\texttt{J\_update}).

\paragraph{Closing the recursion.} Now average the backward conditional
\eqref{eq:sm-backcond} over the already-smoothed next state $\vz_{t+1} \mid
\vy_{1:T} \sim \Normal(\vmu_{t+1}^s, P_{t+1}^s)$, legitimate by the Markov
identity \eqref{eq:sm-markov}. The law of total expectation gives the smoothed
mean; the law of total covariance (conditional covariance plus the covariance of
the conditional mean, $J_t P_{t+1}^s J_t\trp$) gives the smoothed covariance; the
cross term gives the lagged covariance,
\begin{align}
    \vmu_t^s &= \vmu_t + J_t(\vmu_{t+1}^s - \vmu_{t+1}^-), \label{eq:sm-mean}\\
    P_t^s &= P_t + J_t(P_{t+1}^s - P_{t+1}^-) J_t\trp, \label{eq:sm-cov}\\
    P_{t,t+1}^s &= J_t P_{t+1}^s . \label{eq:sm-cross}
\end{align}
The sweep initializes at $t = T$ with $\vmu_T^s = \vmu_T$ and $P_T^s = P_T$: at
the last step ``all the data'' and ``the data so far'' coincide.

\paragraph{Reading the equations.} The smoothed mean \eqref{eq:sm-mean} is the
filtered mean plus a correction $J_t(\vmu_{t+1}^s - \vmu_{t+1}^-)$: the gap
between where the future says the next state landed and where the forward pass
predicted it, pulled back through $J_t$. If the future confirmed the prediction
the correction is zero. Uncertainty can only drop: since $P_{t+1}^s \preceq
P_{t+1}^-$, \eqref{eq:sm-cov} gives $P_t^s \preceq P_t$---smoothing never hurts,
mirroring the filter's $P_t \le P_t^-$. In scalars ($a$ scalar) the gain is $J_t =
a P_t / P_{t+1}^-$, the same shape. The lagged covariance \eqref{eq:sm-cross} is
the second moment $\E[\vz_t \vz_{t+1}\trp \mid \vy_{1:T}]$ that the M-step of EM
needs (the EM route mentioned in \cref{sec:hokalman}); it is what
\texttt{rts\_smoother} returns as \texttt{P\_tp1\_t\_s}. This is the
Rauch--Tung--Striebel smoother \parencite{Rauch1965-rts}; equivalent
forward--backward and two-filter forms exist \parencite{Sarkka2013-bfs}.

\subsection{Forecasting: the filter with the sensor unplugged}

To predict beyond the data, start from the final filtered belief $\Normal(\vmu_T,
P_T)$ and iterate \emph{only} the predict step \eqref{eq:kf-predict} (vector form)
with no update---there are no observations to condition on. Applying it $h$ times
gives the $h$-step-ahead state forecast in closed form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fc-state}
    \vmu_{T+h}^- = A^h \vmu_T, \qquad
    P_{T+h}^- = A^h P_T (A^h)\trp + \sum_{k=0}^{h-1} A^k Q (A^k)\trp .
\end{equation}
Pushing through the sensor $\vy = C\vz + \vv$ gives the predicted observation and
a calibrated predictive interval that widens with $h$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fc-obs}
    \hat{\vy}_{T+h} = C\vmu_{T+h}^-, \qquad
    \cov(\vy_{T+h} \mid \vy_{1:T}) = C P_{T+h}^- C\trp + R .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Steady state.} For a stable $A$ (spectral radius $< 1$) the forecast
mean decays to $\mathbf 0$ and the covariance converges to the stationary
$P_\infty$ solving the discrete Lyapunov equation $P_\infty = A P_\infty A\trp +
Q$; the observation-forecast covariance tends to $C P_\infty C\trp + R$, which is
exactly $\Gamma_0$ of \eqref{eq:hk-markov} (the same $P_\infty$ that opens
\cref{sec:hokalman}). Far ahead, the best guess is just the process mean with
stationary spread. When instead $|\lambda|(A) \geq 1$---the random walk $a = 1$ of
\eqref{eq:kf-model}---the covariance grows without bound: forecasts get vague, as
they should.

\medskip
In one line: the filter is now, the smoother looks back with all the data, the
forecast extrapolates the dynamics---one predict/update pair generates all three.

\begin{exercise}[smoothing never hurts]
Show $P_t^s \preceq P_t$: the smoothed covariance is no larger than the filtered
one.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
From \eqref{eq:sm-cov} it suffices that $P_{t+1}^- \succeq P_{t+1}^s$. Argue by
backward induction. At $t = T$, $P_T^s = P_T \preceq P_T^-$ (the filter update
shrinks the predicted covariance, \eqref{eq:kf-update}). If $P_{t+1}^s \preceq
P_{t+1}^-$, then $J_t(P_{t+1}^s - P_{t+1}^-)J_t\trp \preceq 0$, so
\eqref{eq:sm-cov} gives $P_t^s \preceq P_t$; and $P_t \preceq P_t^-$ again by the
update, closing the induction. Hence smoothing can only reduce uncertainty
relative to filtering.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[long-horizon forecast]
For a stable $A$ (all eigenvalues inside the unit circle), show the $h$-step
state-forecast covariance $P_{T+h}^-$ converges as $h \to \infty$ to the solution
$P_\infty$ of the discrete Lyapunov equation $P_\infty = A P_\infty A\trp + Q$, and
that the observation-forecast covariance tends to $C P_\infty C\trp + R$. Contrast
with the random walk $a = 1$ of \eqref{eq:kf-model}.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
From \eqref{eq:fc-state}, stability makes $A^h \to 0$, so the first term vanishes
and the matrix series converges to $P_\infty = \sum_{k\geq 0} A^k Q (A^k)\trp$,
which satisfies $P_\infty = Q + A P_\infty A\trp$. Applying $C(\cdot)C\trp + R$
gives the stationary observation covariance $C P_\infty C\trp + R = \Gamma_0$ of
\eqref{eq:hk-markov}. For $a = 1$ (unit eigenvalue) the series diverges:
$P_{T+h}^- = P_T + hQ$ grows linearly, so forecasts become arbitrarily uncertain.
\end{solution}

\section{Optional: a map of latent variable models for spike trains}\label{sec:neurallvm}

We have now assembled all the design choices a latent variable model for neural
population activity can make: what the \emph{observations} look like (Gaussian
vs.\ Poisson/point-process, \cref{sec:expfam}), what shape the \emph{latent}
takes (static low-rank factors, a smooth Gaussian-process trajectory, or an
explicit dynamical system), and how \emph{inference} is done (closed-form,
spectral, or approximate/variational). The neuroscience literature is largely a
tour through combinations of these choices. This optional section is a map, not a
derivation: \cref{tab:neurallvm} places a few landmark models as coordinates in
that design space, for further reading.

Two broad families are worth distinguishing. \emph{Gaussian-process} methods
(GPFA \parencite{Yu2009-qp}, vLGP \parencite{Zhao2016a}, and the linear-time
Hida--Mat\'ern approach of \parencite{Dowling2023c}) put a smoothness prior
directly on the latent trajectory without committing to a parametric dynamical
law; \emph{state-space} methods (the linear-Gaussian LDS of \cref{sec:kalman},
the Poisson linear dynamical system \parencite{Macke2011-ep}, and XFADS
\parencite{Dowling2024-xfads}) instead posit an explicit transition model
$\vz_t = f(\vz_{t-1}) + \text{noise}$. Orthogonally, the observation model splits
the closed-form-inference cases (Gaussian emissions: PCA, FA, GPFA, Kalman) from
the ones that lose conjugacy and require approximate inference (point-process /
exponential-family emissions: PLDS, vLGP, cvHM, XFADS), exactly the break
described in \cref{sec:expfam}. Scalability is the third axis: cvHM
\parencite{Dowling2023c} attains linear time in the trajectory length via
Hida--Mat\'ern kernels and conjugate-computation variational inference, and
XFADS scales to large populations with low-rank amortized encoders
(\cref{sec:xfads}). Notebooks \texttt{02}, \texttt{05}, and \texttt{06} implement
representatives of these families.

\section{Optional: learning the dynamics (Ho--Kalman)}\label{sec:hokalman}

\emph{Optional reading, tied to notebook~03.} The filter above assumed the system
$(A, C, Q, R)$ was known. Where do those come from? One route is
probabilistic---EM, exactly as for factor analysis. The other is a fast algebraic
trick from control theory, \emph{subspace identification}; its classic form is the
\emph{Ho--Kalman} algorithm \parencite{Katayama2005-jt}, sketched here. Work at
stationarity, where the latent has settled to $\vz_t \sim \Normal(\mathbf 0,
P_\infty)$ with $P_\infty = A P_\infty A\trp + Q$ (the discrete Lyapunov
equation).

\paragraph{Covariances are Markov parameters.} Compute the output autocovariances
$\Gamma_k = \E[\vy_{t+k}\vy_t\trp]$. Since $\vz_{t+k} = A^k\vz_t + (\text{future
noise} \perp \vz_t)$ we have $\E[\vz_{t+k}\vz_t\trp] = A^k P_\infty$, so for $k \ge
1$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hk-markov}
  \Gamma_k = C A^{k} P_\infty C\trp = C A^{k-1} G, \qquad G := A P_\infty C\trp .
\end{equation}
The lagged covariances behave exactly like the impulse response (``Markov
parameters'') of the deterministic system $(A, C, G)$, so the classical
realization machinery applies.

\paragraph{Hankel, then SVD.} Stack the $\Gamma_k$ ($k\ge1$) into a block-Hankel
matrix whose $(i,j)$ block is $\Gamma_{i+j+1}$. By \eqref{eq:hk-markov} it
\emph{factors} into an observability and a controllability matrix,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hk-factor}
  H = \mathcal{O}\,\mathcal{K},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ CA \\ CA^2 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{K} = \begin{bmatrix} G & AG & A^2 G & \cdots \end{bmatrix} .
\end{equation}
Each factor has only $d$ inner dimensions, so $\rank H \le d$: the latent
dimension is the number of nonzero singular values of $H$---the low-rank story of
PPCA, now on a Hankel of covariances. A truncated SVD $H \approx U_d \Sigma_d
V_d\trp$ splits the two factors, $\mathcal{O} = U_d \Sigma_d^{1/2}$ and
$\mathcal{K} = \Sigma_d^{1/2} V_d\trp$. (The split is unique only up to an
invertible $T$, $\mathcal O \mapsto \mathcal O T$, $\mathcal K \mapsto
T^{-1}\mathcal K$---the basis freedom of the latent coordinates, the rotation
ambiguity of \cref{sec:rotation} again.)

\paragraph{Read off the system.} The observation matrix is the first block row,
$C = \mathcal{O}_{[1]}$. For $A$, use \emph{shift invariance}: the block rows of
$\mathcal O$ obey $\mathcal{O}_{\downarrow} = \mathcal{O}_{\uparrow} A$, where
$\mathcal{O}_{\uparrow}$ drops the last block row and $\mathcal{O}_{\downarrow}$
the first (each block is the one above times $A$). Solving with the pseudoinverse,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hk-A}
  A = \mathcal{O}_{\uparrow}^{\dagger}\, \mathcal{O}_{\downarrow} .
\end{equation}
Finally $G$ is the first block column of $\mathcal K$; $Q$ follows from the
factorization, $P_\infty$ from the Lyapunov equation, and the sensor noise from
$\Gamma_0 = C P_\infty C\trp + R$, i.e.\ $R = \Gamma_0 - C P_\infty C\trp$.

\paragraph{Why bother.} One SVD, no iteration, and out comes a full linear
dynamical system. But it only matches moments---it never touches the
likelihood---so it is statistically less efficient than the EM/ML fit and can
even return an unstable estimate. The usual practice: run Ho--Kalman for a fast,
initialization-free guess, then polish it with EM. Notebook~03 fits both and
compares the eigenvalues of the learned $A$.

\section{Variational inference: when the posterior is no longer Gaussian}\label{sec:vi}

Every model so far handed us the posterior in closed form: PPCA
\eqref{eq:ppca-posterior}, factor analysis \eqref{eq:fa-posterior}, and the Kalman
update \eqref{eq:kf-update} all rest on \cref{fact:cond}, which conditions one
Gaussian on another. That gift has a precondition---prior and likelihood must be
jointly Gaussian (\emph{conjugate}). The moment we left that world, in
\cref{sec:expfam}, the gift was withdrawn. This section names what breaks and how
\emph{variational inference} (VI) recovers a workable posterior by turning
inference into optimization.

\paragraph{The gap.} Bayes' rule reads $p(\vz\mid\vy) = p(\vy\mid\vz)\,p(\vz) /
p(\vy)$ with evidence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vi-evidence}
    p(\vy) = \int p(\vy\mid\vz)\,p(\vz)\,\mathrm{d}\vz .
\end{equation}
Off conjugacy---non-Gaussian observations (spike counts, \cref{sec:expfam}) or
nonlinear dynamics---the pair $(\vz,\vy)$ is no longer jointly Gaussian, the
integral \eqref{eq:vi-evidence} has no closed form, and the posterior is
intractable. Notebook 04 makes the non-conjugacy visible with a 1-D toy (a
Gaussian likelihood times a Laplace prior) evaluated on a grid; grid evaluation is
a luxury of one dimension and dies exponentially as $d$ grows. The strategy: stop
computing $p(\vz\mid\vy)$ exactly and \emph{approximate it by optimization}.

\paragraph{The variational family.} Pick a parametric family $q(\vz;\vphi)$
meant to approximate $p(\vz\mid\vy)$---say Gaussian $q = \Normal(\vmu, \Sigma_q)$
with $\vphi = (\vmu, \Sigma_q)$---that we can sample from, evaluate, and whose
entropy is differentiable. The goal is to choose $\vphi$ so that $q$ is as close as
possible to the true posterior. Closeness needs a divergence.

\paragraph{KL divergence.} The Kullback--Leibler divergence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vi-kl}
    \mathrm{KL}(q \Vert p) = \E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{q(\vz)}{p(\vz)}\right] \geq 0,
    \qquad \text{equality iff } q = p \text{ a.e.}
\end{equation}
is non-negative (Gibbs' inequality) and zero only when the two distributions
agree; it is asymmetric, and reads as the average number of extra nats paid for
coding samples from $q$ under $p$. VI uses the \emph{reverse} form
$\mathrm{KL}(q \Vert p(\cdot\mid\vy))$, which---crucially---needs only the
\emph{unnormalized} posterior $p(\vy,\vz)$, sidestepping the intractable $p(\vy)$.

\paragraph{The ELBO.} Start from $\log p(\vy)$ and multiply-and-divide by $q$
inside the expectation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vi-decomp}
    \log p(\vy) = \underbrace{\E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p(\vy,\vz)}{q(\vz;\vphi)}\right]}_{\mathcal{L}(\vphi)}
        + \underbrace{\E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{q(\vz;\vphi)}{p(\vz\mid\vy)}\right]}_{\mathrm{KL}(q\,\Vert\,p(\cdot\mid\vy))} .
\end{equation}
The first term is the \emph{evidence lower bound} $\mathcal{L}(\vphi)$; the second
is the KL from $q$ to the true posterior. Because that KL is non-negative,
$\mathcal{L}(\vphi) \leq \log p(\vy)$---hence ``lower bound.'' The bound has two
readings,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vi-elbo}
    \mathcal{L}(\vphi) = \E_{q}\!\big[\log p(\vy,\vz) - \log q(\vz;\vphi)\big]
        = \E_{q}\!\big[\log p(\vy\mid\vz)\big] - \mathrm{KL}\!\big(q(\vz;\vphi)\Vert p(\vz)\big) ,
\end{equation}
the second exposing the tension: an expected log-likelihood (reconstruction) pulled
against a KL-to-prior (regularizer). Since $\log p(\vy)$ is a constant in $\vphi$,
raising $\mathcal{L}$ lowers $\mathrm{KL}(q \Vert p(\cdot\mid\vy))$ by exactly the
same amount, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vi-argmax}
    \arg\max_{\vphi}\mathcal{L}(\vphi) = \arg\min_{\vphi}\mathrm{KL}\!\big(q(\vz;\vphi)\Vert p(\vz\mid\vy)\big) .
\end{equation}
Inference has become optimization. Dropping the $\vphi$-constant $\log p(\vy)$, the
objective coded in notebook 04 is $\E_q[\log p(\vy\mid\vz) + \log p(\vz)] + H(q)$,
with $H(q)$ the entropy of $q$.

\paragraph{Monte Carlo and the reparameterization trick.} The ELBO needs the
intractable expectation $\E_q[\cdot]$; estimate it with $n$ samples,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vi-mc}
    \mathcal{L}(\vphi) \approx \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \big[\log p(\vy\mid\vz_i)
        + \log p(\vz_i)\big] + H\!\big(q(\vz;\vphi)\big), \qquad \vz_i \sim q(\vz;\vphi) .
\end{equation}
The obstacle: drawing $\vz_i \sim q$ naively makes each sample a constant in
$\vphi$, so $\nabla_{\vphi}$ does not flow through the sampling. The fix is the
\emph{reparameterization trick}: write the draw as a deterministic, differentiable
function of $\vphi$ plus parameter-free base noise. For a Gaussian,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vi-reparam}
    \vz = \vmu + L\,\bm{\epsilon}, \quad \bm{\epsilon}\sim\Normal(\mathbf 0, I),
    \quad L L\trp = \Sigma_q
    \;\Longrightarrow\;
    \nabla_{\vphi}\E_{q}[f(\vz)] = \E_{\bm{\epsilon}}\!\big[\nabla_{\vphi} f(\vmu + L\bm{\epsilon})\big] ,
\end{equation}
so the draw is Gaussian by \cref{fact:affine} and the gradient now passes through
$\vmu$ and $L$: a low-variance, autodiff-friendly estimator (\texttt{.rsample} in
PyTorch). This applies to location--scale families
\parencite{KingmaWelling2014-vae, Rezende2014-sbp}. When both $q$ and the prior are
Gaussian the KL-to-prior term is closed form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vi-gaussKL}
    \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\Normal(\vmu, \Sigma_q)\Vert\Normal(\mathbf 0, I)\big)
        = \tfrac12\big(\tr\Sigma_q + \vmu\trp\vmu - d - \ln\det\Sigma_q\big) ,
\end{equation}
leaving only the reconstruction term to sample.

\paragraph{Where it goes.} Three payoffs close the section. \emph{Consistency:}
when the model \emph{is} linear-Gaussian, the true posterior is Gaussian, a
Gaussian $q$ reaches $\mathrm{KL} = 0$, and the ELBO optimum reproduces the exact
PPCA/FA/Kalman posterior---VI strictly generalizes the conditioning of
\cref{fact:cond}. \emph{Amortization:} replacing the per-datum $\vphi$ with a
network mapping $\vy$ to variational parameters, $q_{\vphi}(\vz\mid\vy)$, yields the
recognition model, or encoder, of a variational autoencoder (\cref{sec:amortized}).
\emph{XFADS:} applying the recipe to a \emph{sequential} latent---a structured
Gaussian posterior over the whole trajectory $\vz_{1:T}$, an amortized recognition
network, a nonlinear dynamics prior $p(\vz_t\mid\vz_{t-1})$, and a (possibly
Poisson) observation model, all trained by maximizing the ELBO through
reparameterization---is the Kalman world of \cref{sec:kalman} unshackled from
linearity and Gaussianity (\cref{sec:xfads}). Notebooks 05--06 fit a ring
attractor and MC-Maze reaching.

\begin{exercise}[ELBO decomposition]
Starting from $\log p(\vy)$, insert $1 = \int q(\vz;\vphi)\,\mathrm{d}\vz$ and
$p(\vz\mid\vy) = p(\vy,\vz)/p(\vy)$ to prove $\log p(\vy) = \mathcal{L}(\vphi) +
\mathrm{KL}(q(\vz;\vphi)\Vert p(\vz\mid\vy))$. Conclude $\mathcal{L}(\vphi) \leq
\log p(\vy)$, and that maximizing the ELBO minimizes $\mathrm{KL}(q \Vert
p(\cdot\mid\vy))$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
$\log p(\vy) = \E_q[\log p(\vy)] = \E_q\!\big[\log\frac{p(\vy,\vz)}{p(\vz\mid\vy)}\big]
= \E_q\!\big[\log\frac{p(\vy,\vz)}{q(\vz)}\big] + \E_q\!\big[\log\frac{q(\vz)}{p(\vz\mid\vy)}\big]
= \mathcal{L}(\vphi) + \mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p(\cdot\mid\vy))$. Gibbs' inequality gives
$\mathrm{KL}\geq 0$, hence $\mathcal{L}\leq\log p(\vy)$; and since $\log p(\vy)$ is
independent of $\vphi$, raising $\mathcal{L}$ lowers the KL by the same amount.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[VI recovers exact conditioning in the linear-Gaussian case]
For $\vz\sim\Normal(\mathbf 0, I_d)$ and $\vy\mid\vz\sim\Normal(C\vz,
\Sigma_{\veta})$, argue a Gaussian family $q(\vz;\vphi) = \Normal(\vmu, \Sigma_q)$
can drive the ELBO gap to zero, and identify the optimal $(\vmu, \Sigma_q)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
The true posterior is Gaussian (\cref{fact:cond}, \cref{sec:fa}), so it lies inside
the family. Taking $\Sigma_q = M^{-1}$ and $\vmu = M^{-1}C\trp\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}\vy$
with $M = I_d + C\trp\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}C$ (eq.~\eqref{eq:fa-M}) makes $q =
p(\cdot\mid\vy)$, so $\mathrm{KL} = 0$ and $\mathcal{L} = \log p(\vy)$: VI
reproduces the closed-form FA posterior \eqref{eq:fa-posterior} and strictly
generalizes it.
\end{solution}

\section{Amortized inference and the variational autoencoder}\label{sec:amortized}

Every posterior in these notes so far has been a closed-form Gaussian read-off:
PPCA \eqref{eq:ppca-posterior}, FA \eqref{eq:fa-posterior}, the Kalman update
\eqref{eq:kf-update}---all resting on \cref{fact:cond}. What if the observation map
is nonlinear (spikes through a Poisson link, \cref{sec:expfam}) or the dynamical
prior is nonlinear (the van der Pol and ring-attractor systems of the notebooks)?
Then $p(\vz\mid\vy)$ has no closed form and the evidence \eqref{eq:vi-evidence} is
intractable. Two escapes: MCMC (accurate, slow) or variational inference (inference
as optimization, \cref{sec:vi}). We take the second---then make it \emph{scale}.

\paragraph{Variational inference in one screen.} Fit a tractable $q(\vz;
\lambda)$ to $p(\vz\mid\vy)$ by minimizing the reverse KL $\mathrm{KL}(q \Vert
p(\cdot\mid\vy))$. The ELBO identity \eqref{eq:vi-decomp} gives $\log p(\vy) =
\mathcal{L} + \mathrm{KL} \geq \mathcal{L}$: the ELBO lower-bounds the evidence, and
maximizing it both fits $q$ to the posterior and bounds the marginal likelihood for
learning the generative parameters. Its reconstruction-minus-KL form is
\eqref{eq:vi-elbo}. Notebook 04 does exactly this, numerically, for a 1-D toy
posterior. The foundational treatments are \parencite{Jordan1999-vi,
Blei2017-vi}; note the reverse KL is \emph{mode-seeking}, so $q$ prefers to cover
one mode tightly rather than smear across several.

\paragraph{The bottleneck amortization removes.} Classical VI optimizes a fresh
$\lambda$ for every datapoint $\vy$---$N$ inner optimizations for $N$
observations, and a brand-new one for every test trial. Amortized inference
replaces the per-point optimum $\lambda(\vy)$ with a shared function $\lambda =
f_{\phi}(\vy)$: one neural network, trained once across all data, that
\emph{predicts} the variational parameters. This network is the recognition model
(inference network, encoder) \parencite{Gershman2014-amortized}. The trade is an
\emph{amortization gap}---the network need not hit each point's exact optimum---
bought for $O(1)$ test-time inference, a single forward pass.

\paragraph{Recognition network plus reparameterization is a VAE.} Make the
recognition model an amortized Gaussian,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vae-recog}
    q_{\phi}(\vz\mid\vy) = \Normal\!\big(\vmu_{\phi}(\vy),\; \Sigma_{\phi}(\vy)\big) ,
\end{equation}
with mean and (diagonal or Cholesky) covariance emitted by a network. The
reparameterized draw
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vae-reparam}
    \vz = \vmu_{\phi}(\vy) + L_{\phi}(\vy)\,\bm{\epsilon}, \quad
    \bm{\epsilon}\sim\Normal(\mathbf 0, I_d), \quad
    \Sigma_{\phi}(\vy) = L_{\phi}(\vy) L_{\phi}(\vy)\trp
\end{equation}
makes samples differentiable in $\phi$, yielding a low-variance Monte Carlo
estimate of $\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}$. Writing the ELBO with explicit
decoder/encoder parameters,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vae-elbo}
    \log p_{\theta}(\vy) = \mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;\vy)
        + \mathrm{KL}\!\big(q_{\phi}(\vz\mid\vy)\Vert p_{\theta}(\vz\mid\vy)\big)
        \geq \mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;\vy),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vae-elbo2}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;\vy) = \E_{q_{\phi}(\vz\mid\vy)}\!\big[\log p_{\theta}(\vy\mid\vz)\big]
        - \mathrm{KL}\!\big(q_{\phi}(\vz\mid\vy)\Vert p(\vz)\big),
\end{equation}
and jointly maximizing it over encoder $\phi$ and decoder $\theta$ by stochastic
gradient ascent gives the \emph{variational autoencoder}
\parencite{KingmaWelling2014-vae, Rezende2014-sbp}. Notebook 04 ends precisely at
this $q_{\phi}(\vz\mid\vy)$ bridge.

\paragraph{Reconnecting to the linear-Gaussian thread.} The encoder is learning to
imitate the conditioning formula. In the linear-Gaussian case the ideal recognition
map is \emph{known}: it is the affine posterior mean of \eqref{eq:fa-posterior},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vae-lg}
    \vmu_{\phi}(\vy) = M^{-1}C\trp\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}\vy, \qquad
    \Sigma_{\phi} = M^{-1}, \qquad M = I_d + C\trp\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}C,
\end{equation}
so a linear-Gaussian encoder represents the exact posterior with \emph{zero}
amortization gap. Amortized inference generalizes, rather than competes with,
Kalman/FA inference. For a moving latent (\cref{sec:kalman}) one amortizes over
sequences, $q_{\phi}(\vz_{1:T}\mid\vy_{1:T})$: the recognition network plays the
Kalman filter/smoother's role, but learned and usable under nonlinear dynamics and
non-Gaussian observations. This is LFADS \parencite{Pandarinath2018-lfads} and
XFADS \parencite{Dowling2024-xfads}, notebooks 05--06. The recurring caveat
persists: the latent is identifiable only up to an invertible reparameterization
(\cref{sec:rotation}).

\begin{exercise}
Show the ELBO is a genuine lower bound on the log-evidence. Starting from $\log
p_{\theta}(\vy) = \log\E_{q_{\phi}(\vz\mid\vy)}\!\big[p_{\theta}(\vy,\vz)/
q_{\phi}(\vz\mid\vy)\big]$, apply Jensen's inequality to move the log inside the
expectation, and identify the gap with $\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\vz\mid\vy)\Vert
p_{\theta}(\vz\mid\vy))$. When is the bound tight?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Jensen gives $\log\E_q[\cdot] \geq \E_q[\log\cdot] = \E_q[\log p_{\theta}(\vy,\vz) -
\log q_{\phi}(\vz\mid\vy)] = \mathcal{L}$. Writing $p_{\theta}(\vy,\vz) =
p_{\theta}(\vz\mid\vy)\,p_{\theta}(\vy)$ and subtracting shows $\log p_{\theta}(\vy)
- \mathcal{L} = \mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}\Vert p_{\theta}(\vz\mid\vy)) \geq 0$, tight iff
$q_{\phi}(\vz\mid\vy) = p_{\theta}(\vz\mid\vy)$ almost everywhere.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[linear-Gaussian sanity check]
For the PPCA/FA model the exact posterior \eqref{eq:fa-posterior} is Gaussian with
mean linear in $\vy$. Show an amortized Gaussian recognition network with affine
mean $\vmu_{\phi}(\vy) = W\vy$ and constant covariance can represent it exactly
(zero amortization gap), and give $W$. What does this say about VAEs relative to
Kalman/FA inference?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Matching \eqref{eq:fa-posterior}, take $W = M^{-1}C\trp\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}$ and
constant $\Sigma_{\phi} = M^{-1}$ with $M = I_d + C\trp\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}C$; the
network reproduces the true posterior for every $\vy$. Hence amortized inference
strictly generalizes closed-form Gaussian conditioning: when the model is
linear-Gaussian the optimal encoder is affine and recovers the Kalman/FA update,
and nonlinear $\vmu_{\phi}, \Sigma_{\phi}$ are needed only when the generative
model is nonlinear.
\end{solution}

\section{Nonlinear state-space models: XFADS}\label{sec:xfads}

Recall the arc. The Kalman filter (\cref{sec:kalman}) kept a Gaussian belief exact
because every arrow was linear-Gaussian; Ho--Kalman (\cref{sec:hokalman}) recovered
the linear system. Two things break for real neural data: the latent dynamics are
\emph{nonlinear} (ring attractors, limit cycles---notebooks 05--06), and spike
counts are \emph{non-Gaussian} (Poisson, not $\vy = C\vz + \text{noise}$). XFADS
\parencite{Dowling2024-xfads} keeps as much of the Kalman/Gaussian structure as it
can while relaxing both. One-line framing: a variational autoencoder whose decoder
is a state-space model and whose encoder does structured Gaussian smoothing.

\paragraph{The generative model: nonlinear Gaussian state space.} Replace the
linear map $A\vz_{t-1}$ of \eqref{eq:kf-model} with a nonlinear mean function
$f_{\theta}$ (a GRU \parencite{Cho2014-gru} or MLP), keeping Gaussian process
noise, and replace the Gaussian sensor with an exponential-family emission whose
natural parameter is the linear read-out $C\vz_t + \vb$ of \cref{sec:expfam},
\begin{genmodel}
\begin{align}
    \vz_t &= f_{\theta}(\vz_{t-1}) + \ve_t, \quad \ve_t\sim\Normal(\mathbf 0, Q),
        \qquad \vz_1\sim\Normal(\vm_0, P_0), \label{eq:xf-dyn}\\
    \vy_t &\sim p(\vy_t \mid \vz_t), \quad\text{e.g.}\quad
        \vy_t \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\!\big(\exp(C\vz_t + \vb)\big) \text{ componentwise},
        \label{eq:xf-obs}
\end{align}
\end{genmodel}
with the Gaussian case $\vy_t\sim\Normal(C\vz_t, R)$ recovering the Kalman sensor
exactly. This is precisely the Kalman generative model with $A\vz_{t-1}$ swapped for
$f_{\theta}(\vz_{t-1})$ and the Gaussian likelihood swapped for an exp-family one
(the code's \texttt{DenseGaussianDynamics} and \texttt{GaussianLikelihood}/Poisson
modules).

\paragraph{Why exact inference breaks, and the fix.} Once $f_{\theta}$ is nonlinear
or the likelihood non-Gaussian, the posterior $p(\vz_{1:T}\mid\vy_{1:T})$ is no
longer Gaussian and \cref{fact:cond} no longer closes the recursion. Two escapes
from the notes' vocabulary: \emph{linearize} (extended/unscented Kalman---brittle)
or do \emph{variational inference} (\cref{sec:vi}: turn Bayes into optimization).
XFADS takes the VI route but insists the variational family keep the
temporal-correlation structure a smoother needs.

\paragraph{A structured Gaussian posterior.} Define the approximate posterior as a
multivariate Gaussian over the whole trajectory with a Gauss--Markov
(block-tridiagonal precision) structure,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xf-post}
    q_{\phi}(\vz_{1:T}\mid\vy_{1:T}) = \Normal(\vmu, \Sigma), \quad
    J = \Sigma^{-1} \text{ block-tridiagonal (blocks } J_{t,t}, J_{t,t+1}\text{)} ,
\end{equation}
a \emph{full smoothing} posterior, not a mean-field-across-time factorization. (LFADS
\parencite{Pandarinath2018-lfads} uses a GRU generator but an
initial-condition-driven posterior; deep Kalman filters \parencite{Krishnan2017-dkf}
use mean-field-in-time inference.) The block-tridiagonal precision is the
information-form counterpart of the Kalman smoother.

\paragraph{Natural parameters add.} Parameterize each Gaussian by its natural
(information) form: precision $J = \Sigma^{-1}$ and information vector $h =
\Sigma^{-1}\vmu$, with density factor $\propto \exp(-\tfrac12\vz\trp J\vz +
h\trp\vz)$. The key fact, carried straight from the ``precisions add'' reading of
\eqref{eq:kf-bayes}: multiplying Gaussian factors \emph{adds} their natural
parameters. XFADS writes the posterior natural parameters at each $t$ as an
additive decomposition,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xf-additive}
    (h_t, J_t) = (h_t^{\mathrm{dyn}}, J_t^{\mathrm{dyn}})
        + (h_t^{\mathrm{loc}}, J_t^{\mathrm{loc}})
        + (h_t^{\mathrm{bwd}}, J_t^{\mathrm{bwd}}) ,
\end{equation}
the generalized information-filter update: the dynamics supply the predicted term,
and two encoders supply data-dependent \emph{pseudo-observation} terms. Where a
linear-Gaussian sensor would contribute $C\trp R^{-1} C$ and $C\trp R^{-1}\vy_t$, a
neural network now supplies those pieces.

\paragraph{Local and backward encoders.} Two amortized networks build the data
terms. The \emph{local} encoder $\alpha_{\phi}$ maps each $\vy_t$ to a per-timestep
Gaussian potential in natural form, with a low-rank-plus-identity precision so it
stays cheap at large latent dimension,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xf-local}
    (h_t^{\mathrm{loc}}, L_t) = \alpha_{\phi}(\vy_t), \qquad
    J_t^{\mathrm{loc}} = L_t L_t\trp, \quad L_t\in\reals^{d\times r},\ r\ll d
\end{equation}
(code: \texttt{LocalEncoderLRMvn}, ``LR'' = low rank). This is the nonlinear
analogue of factor analysis's ``precision-weight the observation before reading it
into latent space,'' $C\trp\Sigma_{\veta}^{-1}\vy$. The \emph{backward} encoder
$\beta_{\phi}$ is a reverse-time GRU sweeping the local potentials from $T$ down to
$t$, summarizing the future $\vy_{t+1:T}$ into a message,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xf-backward}
    (h_t^{\mathrm{bwd}}, L_t^{\mathrm{bwd}}) = \beta_{\phi}(\vy_{t+1:T}), \qquad
    J_t^{\mathrm{bwd}} = L_t^{\mathrm{bwd}} (L_t^{\mathrm{bwd}})\trp .
\end{equation}
This is what upgrades filtering to smoothing: the forward dynamics prediction
supplies the past, the backward encoder supplies the future, and their natural
parameters add---an amortized, learned two-filter/information smoother. The belief
at $t$ is the sum \eqref{eq:xf-additive}, sampled by reparameterization.

\paragraph{Training: one ELBO, end to end.} The objective is the sequence ELBO
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xf-elbo}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi) = \sum_{t=1}^T \E_{q}\!\big[\log p(\vy_t\mid\vz_t)\big]
        - \mathrm{KL}\!\big(q_{\phi}(\vz_{1:T}\mid\vy_{1:T})\Vert p_{\theta}(\vz_{1:T})\big) ,
\end{equation}
the expected exponential-family log-likelihood minus the KL between the structured
Gaussian posterior and the nonlinear Gaussian prior, Monte Carlo estimated with
reparameterized samples (notebook 04's ELBO, now over trajectories). All of
$f_{\theta}$, $C$, $\vb$, $Q$, and the two encoders are trained jointly by
stochastic gradient ascent---``amortized'' because the encoders replace
per-sequence optimization with a single forward pass.

\paragraph{Where it sits.} XFADS descends from a line of structured
variational methods for state-space models: black-box VI for SSMs
\parencite{Archer2015-bbvi}, structured VAEs that compose graphical models with
networks \parencite{Johnson2016-svae}, deep Kalman filters
\parencite{Krishnan2017-dkf}, and LFADS \parencite{Pandarinath2018-lfads}. Its
contribution is to keep a genuinely structured (block-tridiagonal) Gaussian
smoothing posterior while scaling to large latent and neuron counts through
low-rank natural-parameter encoders. Notebook 05 recovers a ring attractor's flow
field; notebook 06 fits MC-Maze spikes.

\begin{exercise}[Gaussian products add in natural coordinates]
Two Gaussian factors over $\vz\in\reals^d$, proportional to $\exp(-\tfrac12
\vz\trp J_1\vz + h_1\trp\vz)$ and $\exp(-\tfrac12\vz\trp J_2\vz + h_2\trp\vz)$, are
multiplied. Show the product is proportional to a Gaussian with precision $J_1 +
J_2$ and information vector $h_1 + h_2$, hence mean $(J_1 + J_2)^{-1}(h_1 + h_2)$.
This is the scalar ``precisions add'' rule of \eqref{eq:kf-bayes} in $d$
dimensions, and it is why XFADS can add the dynamics, local, and backward terms.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Multiplying the exponentials and collecting terms in $\vz$: the quadratic term is
$-\tfrac12\vz\trp(J_1 + J_2)\vz$ and the linear term is $(h_1 + h_2)\trp\vz$, the
natural form of a Gaussian with precision $J_1 + J_2$ and information $h_1 + h_2$;
completing the square gives mean $(J_1 + J_2)^{-1}(h_1 + h_2)$. Normalization is
absorbed into the proportionality constant.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[XFADS reduces to the Kalman information filter]
Take $f_{\theta}$ linear, $f_{\theta}(\vz) = A\vz$, and a Gaussian likelihood
$\vy_t\sim\Normal(C\vz_t, R)$. Show the ideal local pseudo-observation natural
parameters are $J_t^{\mathrm{loc}} = C\trp R^{-1} C$ and $h_t^{\mathrm{loc}} =
C\trp R^{-1}\vy_t$, and that adding them to the predicted information reproduces
the vector Kalman update in information form. Conclude the encoders learn a
nonlinear, amortized stand-in for $C\trp R^{-1} C$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
$\log\Normal(\vy_t; C\vz_t, R) = -\tfrac12(\vy_t - C\vz_t)\trp R^{-1}(\vy_t -
C\vz_t) + \mathrm{const}$ is quadratic in $\vz_t$; its second-derivative
(precision) contribution is $C\trp R^{-1} C$ and its linear (information)
contribution is $C\trp R^{-1}\vy_t$. By the previous exercise these add to the
predicted-belief natural parameters $J_t^-, h_t^-$, giving $J_t = J_t^- + C\trp
R^{-1} C$ and $h_t = h_t^- + C\trp R^{-1}\vy_t$---the information-form Kalman
update, equivalent to \eqref{eq:kf-update} (Woodbury converts between covariance
and information forms).
\end{solution}
