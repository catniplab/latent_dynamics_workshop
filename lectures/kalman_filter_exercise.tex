\section*{1. Intuition: hidden state and noisy observations}

Suppose a sensor measures the position of an object on a line. The sensor is
noisy, so the raw readings $y_t$ jump around. The sensor noise variance is $R$.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Suppose $R=4$. If you only think about the next raw observation $y_5$,
  what noise variance do you expect for this next sensor reading?

  \vspace{3em}

  \begin{solution}
  Each raw reading carries the same sensor noise, so
  $\operatorname{Var}(y_5)=R=4$. Seeing past readings does not change the noise
  of the \emph{next} raw measurement.
  \end{solution}

  \item Suppose we have strong reason to believe the object is stationary. Then
  previous readings can tell us where the fixed position is likely to be. Let
  $\mu_T$ be the average of the first $T$ readings:
  \[
  \mu_T = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T y_t.
  \]
  If the readings are independent and each has noise variance $R$, what is
  $P_T=\operatorname{Var}(\mu_T)$?

  (Note that this is different from the sample variance of the four readings)

  \vspace{3em}

  \begin{solution}
  The average of $T$ independent readings, each with variance $R$, has
  \[
  P_T=\operatorname{Var}(\mu_T)
     =\frac{1}{T^2}\sum_{t=1}^T \operatorname{Var}(y_t)
     =\frac{1}{T^2}\,(T R)=\frac{R}{T}.
  \]
  More readings shrink the variance of the estimate by $1/T$.
  \end{solution}

  \item Suppose
  \[
  y_1=7,\qquad y_2=9,\qquad y_3=8,\qquad y_4=8.
  \]
  Compute the belief mean and variance after four readings, using $R=4$.
  \[
  \mu_4 =
  \hspace{5cm}
  P_4 =
  \hspace{5cm}
  \]

  \vspace{1.4em}

  \begin{solution}
  $\mu_4=\tfrac{1}{4}(7+9+8+8)=\tfrac{32}{4}=8$, and
  $P_4=\dfrac{R}{4}=\dfrac{4}{4}=1$.
  \end{solution}

  \item A new reading arrives: $y_5=10$. Update the average without recomputing
  the whole sum:
  \[
  \mu_5
  =
  \mu_4+\alpha(y_5-\mu_4).
  \]
  What is $\alpha$? What is $\mu_5$?
  \[
  \alpha =
  \hspace{5cm}
  \mu_5 =
  \hspace{5cm}
  \]

  \vspace{1.4em}

  \begin{solution}
  The running average of five readings weights the new one by
  $\alpha=\tfrac{1}{5}$, so
  $\mu_5=8+\tfrac{1}{5}(10-8)=8.4$ (check: $\tfrac{32+10}{5}=8.4$). Equivalently
  $\alpha=\dfrac{P_4}{P_4+R}=\dfrac{1}{1+4}=\tfrac{1}{5}$: the gain form already.
  \end{solution}

  \item Suppose we have observed $N$ readings. We can compute the average
  $\mu_N$ and its variance. Now a new reading arrives: $y_{N+1}$. How should we
  update the average? How is the update weight $\alpha$ related to the variance
  of the average and the variance of the sensor noise?

  \vspace{3em}

  \begin{solution}
  With $P_N=R/N$ the belief variance after $N$ readings,
  \[
  \mu_{N+1}=\mu_N+\alpha\,(y_{N+1}-\mu_N),
  \qquad
  \alpha=\frac{1}{N+1}=\frac{P_N}{P_N+R}.
  \]
  The weight is the ratio of the belief variance to belief-plus-sensor
  variance: a confident belief (small $P_N$) gives a small $\alpha$ and barely
  moves; an uncertain belief (large $P_N$) trusts the new reading.
  \end{solution}
\end{enumerate}

\section*{2. Intuition: choosing the gain from uncertainty}
Now write the same idea as a belief update. Before seeing a new observation, suppose
\[
  z\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_b,P_b),
  \qquad
  y\mid z\sim \mathcal{N}(z,R)
\]
A natural update is:
\[
  \mu_{\text{new}}=\mu_b+K(y-\mu_b) \qquad\qquad K=\frac{P_b}{P_b+R}
\]

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Check the limiting cases.
  \[
    R\to 0
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad
    K\to
    \hspace{3cm}
    R\to \infty
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad
    K\to
    \hspace{3cm}
  \]

  \vspace{1.4em}

  \[
    P_b\to 0
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad
    K\to
    \hspace{3cm}
    P_b\to \infty
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad
    K\to
    \hspace{3cm}
  \]

  \vspace{1.8em}

  \begin{solution}
  A perfect sensor or a hopeless prior means trust the data
  ($R\to0\Rightarrow K\to1$, $P_b\to\infty\Rightarrow K\to1$); a hopeless sensor
  or a perfect prior means ignore the data
  ($R\to\infty\Rightarrow K\to0$, $P_b\to0\Rightarrow K\to0$).
  \end{solution}

  \item Derive the variance update from the mean update.

  \vspace{5cm}

  \begin{solution}
  Combining two independent Gaussian sources of information adds precisions
  (inverse variances):
  \[
  \frac{1}{P_{\text{new}}}=\frac{1}{P_b}+\frac{1}{R}
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  P_{\text{new}}=\frac{P_b R}{P_b+R}.
  \]
  This equals the gain form: with $K=\dfrac{P_b}{P_b+R}$,
  \[
  (1-K)P_b=\Bigl(\frac{R}{P_b+R}\Bigr)P_b=\frac{P_b R}{P_b+R}=P_{\text{new}}.
  \]
  \end{solution}

  \item Suppose:
  \[
  \mu_b = 8, \qquad P_b = 4, \qquad y = 10, \qquad R = 1.
  \]
  Is the new mean closer to $8$ or to $10$? Explain using uncertainty.

  \vspace{2.0em}

  Compute $K$, $\mu_{\text{new}}$, and $P_{\text{new}}$.
  \[
  K =
  \hspace{3cm}
  \mu_{\text{new}} =
  \hspace{3cm}
  P_{\text{new}} =
  \hspace{3cm}
  \]

  \vspace{2.0em}

  \begin{solution}
  The belief ($P_b=4$) is more uncertain than the sensor ($R=1$), so the update
  leans toward the measurement $10$:
  \[
  K=\frac{4}{4+1}=0.8,\quad
  \mu_{\text{new}}=8+0.8\,(10-8)=9.6,\quad
  P_{\text{new}}=(1-0.8)\cdot4=0.8.
  \]
  \end{solution}

\end{enumerate}

\section*{3. Bayesian interpretation and the MAP solution}

Now we give the same update a Bayesian reading. The hidden position $z$ is not
observed directly. Before seeing the new measurement, our belief is the
\textbf{prior}:
\[
z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_b,P_b),
\qquad
p(z)\propto
\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(z-\mu_b)^2}{P_b}\right].
\]

The sensor model says that, if the true position were $z$, the measurement
would be centered at $z$ with noise variance $R$. This is the
\textbf{likelihood}:
\[
y\mid z \sim \mathcal{N}(z,R),
\qquad
p(y\mid z)\propto
\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(y-z)^2}{R}\right].
\]

Bayes' rule says to multiply the likelihood by the prior:
\[
p(z \mid y)
\propto
p(y\mid z)p(z)
\propto
\exp\left[
-\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\frac{(z-\mu_b)^2}{P_b}
+
\frac{(y-z)^2}{R}
\right)
\right].
\]

Define the quadratic cost:
\[
J(z)=
\frac{(z-\mu_b)^2}{P_b}
+
\frac{(y-z)^2}{R}.
\]
The \textbf{MAP(Maximum A Posteriori) estimate} is the value of $z$ that makes the posterior largest:
\[
z_{\mathrm{MAP}}=\arg\max_z p(z\mid y).
\]
Because $p(z\mid y)\propto \exp[-J(z)/2]$, maximizing the posterior is the same
as minimizing this quadratic cost:
\[
z_{\mathrm{MAP}}=\arg\min_z J(z).
\]

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Find the MAP estimate by differentiating $J(z)$ and setting the derivative to zero. With $K=P_b/(P_b+R)$, show that this is the same mean update as before:
  \[
  z_{\mathrm{MAP}}
  =
  \mu_b+K(y-\mu_b).
  \]

  \vspace{5.0cm}

  \begin{solution}
  Set $J'(z)=0$:
  \[
  \frac{2(z-\mu_b)}{P_b}-\frac{2(y-z)}{R}=0
  \;\Rightarrow\;
  R(z-\mu_b)=P_b(y-z)
  \;\Rightarrow\;
  z=\frac{R\mu_b+P_b y}{P_b+R}.
  \]
  Rewriting with $K=\dfrac{P_b}{P_b+R}$,
  \[
  z=\mu_b+\frac{P_b}{P_b+R}(y-\mu_b)=\mu_b+K(y-\mu_b).
  \]
  \end{solution}

  \item The posterior is Gaussian. For a Gaussian posterior, the MAP estimate
  and the posterior mean are the same, so $z_{\mathrm{MAP}}=\mu_{\text{new}}$.
  The variance comes from the curvature of $J(z)$:
  \[
  \frac{1}{P_{\text{new}}}
  =
  \hspace{5em}
  \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
  P_{\text{new}}
  =
  \hspace{5em}
  \]

  Fill in the gain form:
  \[
  P_{\text{new}}
  =
  (1-K)P_b.
  \]

  \vspace{2.0em}

  \begin{solution}
  The curvature of $J$ is $J''(z)=\dfrac{2}{P_b}+\dfrac{2}{R}$, and for a
  Gaussian the posterior precision is half of it:
  \[
  \frac{1}{P_{\text{new}}}=\frac{1}{P_b}+\frac{1}{R}
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  P_{\text{new}}=\frac{P_b R}{P_b+R}=(1-K)P_b.
  \]
  \end{solution}
\end{enumerate}

\stepbox{
\textbf{Bayesian interpretation.}
For Gaussian belief and Gaussian sensor noise, the MAP solution gives exactly
the same gain update:
\[
K=\frac{P_b}{P_b+R},
\qquad
\mu_{\text{new}}=\mu_b+K(y-\mu_b),
\qquad
P_{\text{new}}=(1-K)P_b.
\]
The number $K$ tells how strongly the observation pulls the belief.
}

\section*{4. Moving target: the 1D Kalman filter}

A Kalman filter repeats two steps: first move the belief forward with a model,
then update it using the new measurement. For a simple moving target:
\[
z_t = az_{t-1}+e_t,
\qquad
e_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q),
\qquad
y_t=z_t+v_t,
\qquad
v_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R).
\]

Before using $y_t$, the model gives:
\[
\mu_t^-=a\mu_{t-1},
\qquad
P_t^-=a^2P_{t-1}+Q.
\]

Then reuse the single-measurement update from Section 2:
\[
K_t=\frac{P_t^-}{P_t^-+R},
\qquad
\mu_t=\mu_t^-+K_t(y_t-\mu_t^-),
\qquad
P_t=(1-K_t)P_t^-.
\]

\textbf{Exercise.} Use
\[
\mu_0=0,\qquad P_0=2,\qquad a=1,\qquad Q=2,\qquad R=4,
\]
\[
y_1=2,\qquad y_2=2,\qquad y_3=5.
\]

For each row, first compute the model-based belief $(\mu_t^-,P_t^-)$, then
update it with the measurement $y_t$. Keep three decimal places if needed.

{\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{2.25}
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c|c}
t & y_t & \mu_t^- & P_t^- & K_t & \mu_t & P_t \\
\hline
1 & 2 & \hspace{4.2em} & \hspace{4.2em} & \hspace{4.2em} &
\hspace{4.2em} & \hspace{4.2em} \\
2 & 2 & & & & & \\
3 & 5 & & & & &
\end{array}
\]
}

\vspace{2.0em}

Show the update for time step 1:
\[
\mu_1^-=
\qquad
P_1^-=
\qquad
K_1=
\]
\[
\mu_1=
\qquad
P_1=
\]

\vspace{2.5em}

\begin{solution}
Step 1: $\mu_1^-=a\mu_0=0$, $P_1^-=a^2P_0+Q=2+2=4$,
$K_1=\dfrac{4}{4+4}=0.5$, $\mu_1=0+0.5(2-0)=1$, $P_1=(1-0.5)\cdot4=2$.
The variance reaches its steady state ($P^-=4$, $K=0.5$, $P=2$) at once, so
every row shares the same gain:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c|c}
t & y_t & \mu_t^- & P_t^- & K_t & \mu_t & P_t \\
\hline
1 & 2 & 0    & 4 & 0.5 & 1    & 2 \\
2 & 2 & 1    & 4 & 0.5 & 1.5  & 2 \\
3 & 5 & 1.5  & 4 & 0.5 & 3.25 & 2
\end{array}
\]
\end{solution}

\textbf{Interpret the result.}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item At time step 3, the sensor observation jumps to $5$. Does the belief
  jump all the way to $5$? Why or why not?

  \begin{solution}
  No: $\mu_3=3.25$, not $5$. With $K=0.5$ the belief moves only halfway from the
  prediction $\mu_3^-=1.5$ toward the observation, $1.5+0.5(5-1.5)=3.25$. The
  noisy measurement is trusted only partly because the prediction still carries
  information.
  \end{solution}

  \blankline
  \blankline

  \item Suppose the sensor becomes much noisier, so $R=16$ instead of $R=4$.
  Would the measurement weight become larger or smaller? What would that do to
  the new mean?

  \begin{solution}
  Smaller. $K_t=\dfrac{P_t^-}{P_t^-+R}$ shrinks as $R$ grows, so the update
  moves the mean less toward the observation and the belief stays closer to the
  model prediction.
  \end{solution}

  \blankline
  \blankline

  \item In one sentence, answer the main filtering question: how does the filter
  reconcile disagreement between observation and belief?

  \begin{solution}
  It sets the estimate to a precision-weighted average of the model prediction
  and the new measurement, leaning toward whichever is more certain, with the
  Kalman gain fixing the balance.
  \end{solution}

  \blankline
  \blankline
\end{enumerate}

\section*{5. Optional harder extension: two-dimensional position}

Now the hidden state is a vector:
\[
\mathbf{z}_t =
\begin{bmatrix}
z_{1,t} \\
z_{2,t}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Use the vector version of the moving-target model, where the sensor measures
both coordinates directly:
\[
\mathbf{z}_t=A\mathbf{z}_{t-1}+\mathbf{e}_t,
\qquad
\mathbf{y}_t=\mathbf{z}_t+\mathbf{v}_t.
\]

The two-dimensional equations are the vector version of the 1D belief update:
\[
\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^- = A\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t-1},
\qquad
P_t^- = AP_{t-1}A^\top+Q,
\]
\[
\mathbf{d}_t = \mathbf{y}_t-\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^-,
\qquad
S_t = P_t^- + R,
\qquad
K_t = P_t^- S_t^{-1},
\]
\[
\boldsymbol{\mu}_t =
\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^- + K_t\mathbf{d}_t,
\qquad
P_t = (I-K_t)P_t^-.
\]

\[
